Definition

A molecular network, in MS/MS analysis, is a graph whose nodes represent spectra or features with associated spectra and whose edges encode selected relationships. In feature-based molecular networking, feature detection and alignment retain chromatographic context and link representative MS/MS to quantitative information. Separate measured features can remain separate even when their spectra resemble one another. [1]

A chain does not establish endpoint identity

Hypothetical example: A–B scores 0.85, B–C scores 0.81, and A–C scores 0.40. A chosen 0.75 cutoff, with other requirements satisfied, produces the chain A–B–C. All three belong to one connected component, but the first and last spectra are not a high-scoring pair. A supported annotation at A does not identify C.

Network construction may also impose minimum matched peaks, reciprocal neighbor rankings, or component-size restrictions. An above-threshold pair can disappear after these additional rules. Preserve the edge list and settings rather than relying on a screenshot. [2]

Common confusion

An edge is not a chemical bond or proof of a metabolic conversion. A node need not be a unique neutral molecule: several adducts may produce several nodes. Layout proximity is not a calibrated structural distance, and a large drawn node means only what its visual encoding specifies. Missing edges can reflect missing MS/MS, filtering, or incompatible fragmentation rather than unrelated chemistry.

Check your understanding

What makes a network interpretable?

Explicit node and edge definitions, traceable spectra and feature mapping, a clear visual legend, and annotations that retain their evidence and uncertainty.
