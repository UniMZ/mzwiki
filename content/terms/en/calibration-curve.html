Definition

A calibration curve expresses the relationship between an instrument indication and a corresponding measured quantity value. In quantitative MS, it commonly relates analyte concentration to peak area or an analyte/internal-standard response ratio. VIM cautions that the curve alone does not include measurement uncertainty. [1] State the axes and units: a concentration-calibration curve is different from a curve that assigns m/z.

Constructed example

Suppose the response ratio obeys y = 0.020 + 0.050c, with c in ng/mL and a supported interval of 1–10 ng/mL. A ratio of 0.320 gives c = (0.320 − 0.020) / 0.050 = 6.0 ng/mL. The slope has units mL/ng. This inverse calculation assumes the model applies to the unknown; the arithmetic does not validate that assumption.

What the curve does not establish

A high R2 is insufficient evidence of an adequate model. Inspect residual patterns and low-level performance rather than judging only the visual straightness of the plotted points. [2] Standards used to fit a curve do not replace independent quality checks. Do not silently extrapolate beyond the established range, force a zero intercept, or transfer one compound's response factor to another. For dilution and lower-range decisions, continue with the quantitative calibration Guide.
