Definition

A matrix effect is a change in analytical response caused by accompanying sample constituents. In LC-MS, this often concerns ion suppression or enhancement. It differs from losing analyte during extraction and from a separate interfering peak being integrated as analyte. Experiments can distinguish matrix-dependent response from preparation recovery. [1]

A simple example

Constructed matched comparison: an analyte in neat solution gives area 1000; the same nominal final amount added to extracted blank matrix gives 800. The matrix factor is MF = 800 / 1000 = 0.80, corresponding to 20% lower response. [2] With area 1200, MF would be 1.20, or 20% enhancement. State the formula: “matrix effect percent” is used with different conventions.

Common confusion

A factor of 0.80 does not say that 20% of the molecules were physically removed. Nor does high extraction recovery establish absence of matrix effects. Match solvent, amount, injection, and matrix comparisons; account for endogenous analyte and interference when applicable.

A matrix factor measured in one source of material need not characterize all samples. An internal standard can compensate only to the extent that its response tracks the relevant change. A clean-looking chromatogram and a narrow m/z window do not prove that source-level suppression was absent.
