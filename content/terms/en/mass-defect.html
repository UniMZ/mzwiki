Definition

In mass spectrometry, mass defect compares a specified monoisotopic mass with the corresponding nominal mass. Here the signed convention is Δ = monoisotopic mass − nominal mass. State that order whenever reporting a value; an opposite subtraction reverses its sign. Nominal mass is based on integer isotope mass numbers, not an average atomic weight. [1]

A simple example

For a neutral 1H atom, Δ = 1.00782503223 − 1 = +0.00782503223 Da. For neutral 16O, Δ = 15.99491461957 − 16 = −0.00508538043 Da. [2] [3] These calculated examples show why mass defect is not always positive, and why it is not simply the digits after a decimal point.

Common confusion

Mass measurement error compares an observation with a theoretical value; mass defect compares two mass conventions for a composition. The nuclear-physics use compares a bound system with its separated constituents and relates that difference to binding energy. It is a different calculation. [4] In formula inference, a compatible mass defect narrows composition possibilities but cannot establish connectivity. Specify ion form and charge rather than treating an m/z fractional part as a neutral-molecule mass defect.

Check your understanding

Does a negative mass defect show bad calibration?

No. The oxygen example is negative by definition, even with perfectly known isotope mass.
