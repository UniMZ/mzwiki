Definition

A library match links a query spectrum to a reference entry after a search's candidate and acceptance rules have been applied. The useful result includes the reference accession, score definition, matched-peak count, precursor relationship, and experimental metadata. It is more informative than a compound name copied from the first result row. Search tools can expose several hits per spectrum; retaining alternatives makes review possible. [1]

One result, several questions

Hypothetical example: a positive-mode [M+H]+ query returns candidate A at cosine 0.92 with six matched peaks and candidate B at 0.90 with seven. A is first under that scoring rule. The small difference does not establish that A is uniquely correct, and a greater matched-peak count does not automatically make B preferable. Inspect which peaks distinguish them, unmatched signal, adduct compatibility, and possible mixed spectra.

Keep separate records for the raw comparison and the interpretation: “A passed the search threshold” is not equivalent to “A was confirmed with an authentic standard.” If the real compound is missing from the searched collection, the highest-scoring available entry can still be wrong.

Common confusion

A cosine of 0.92 does not mean 92% correctness. Error estimation depends on the method, data, candidate collection, and accepted set; research on small-molecule matching demonstrates project-dependent behavior. [2] State an exact-compound or analog search explicitly, and preserve unresolved alternatives.

Check your understanding

What should accompany a reported name?

The query identifier, reference accession and release, ion assignment, search settings, supporting spectrum, alternatives, and evidence-specific confidence statement.
