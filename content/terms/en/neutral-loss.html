Definition

A neutral loss occurs when ion dissociation releases an uncharged species. [1] The residual charged product can be measured in an ordinary product-ion spectrum; the departing neutral does not produce its own ion peak. Its mass is inferred from an assigned reaction relationship.

A simple example

Constructed calculation: suppose a +2 precursor at m/z 301.100000 loses only neutral 1H216O and remains +2. Treating NIST isotope-mass central values as fixed teaching constants without propagating uncertainties, water is 2 × 1.00782503223 + 15.99491461957 = 18.01056468403 Da. [2] [3] The coordinate decreases by 18.01056468403 / 2 = 9.005282342015, giving product m/z 292.0947 after rounding. The starting coordinate is an exact teaching input, not a measured peak.

Common confusion

For a simple single-loss channel retaining charge magnitude n, neutral mass / Da = n × (precursor m/z − product m/z). This assumes correctly assigned isotope compositions and no additional exchanged or lost species. It does not apply unchanged when the charge changes. An 18.0106 coordinate gap corresponds to about 36.0212 Da if both ions are +2.

A compatible water-loss mass does not identify one structure or locate the original atoms. Nor does a matching gap between unrelated peaks establish a precursor–product relationship. “Neutral loss” names a process; the observed peak belongs to the residual ion.
