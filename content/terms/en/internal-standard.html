Definition

An internal standard is a reference substance added to standards, quality controls, and unknown samples so that its measured response can help quantify an analyte. Its identity, amount, addition stage, and suitability must be specified. In regulated drug bioanalysis, ICH M10 recommends stable-isotope-labelled analyte standards for MS when possible, with checks for purity, interference, and isotope exchange. [1] A labelled compound is not automatically suitable merely because its mass differs.

Constructed example

Two samples receive the same amount of internal standard. Analyte/standard areas are 30,000/15,000 and 18,000/9,000, so both response ratios equal 2. In a stipulated model where the second measurement has a shared response multiplier of 0.6, division cancels that multiplier. It does not prove that the samples contain 2 ng/mL or any other concentration: a calibration relationship is still needed.

Shared variation is the key assumption

A disturbance affecting only the analyte will not cancel in the ratio. An internal standard added late cannot represent earlier changes it never experienced. Monitor its raw response as well as the ratio; an unusual denominator can make a ratio unstable or misleading. One standard should not be assumed to track every analyte in an untargeted experiment. See quantitative calibration for the distinction between a response ratio and a reported quantity.
