Definition

Double bond equivalent (DBE), also called a ring-and-double-bond equivalent, summarizes unsaturation from atom counts under stated valence assumptions. For a connected, closed-shell neutral molecule with tetravalent C, trivalent N, divalent O, and monovalent H and halogens X, use DBE = 1 + C − (H + X)/2 + N/2. The symbols represent atom counts. Oxygen does not enter this expression. [1]

A simple example

The constructed formula C8H10N4O2 gives 1 + 8 − 10/2 + 4/2 = 6. Under the assumptions above, DBE counts rings plus double bonds plus twice triple bonds. Thus 6 is a combined constraint, not a declaration of six double bonds or a particular ring system. Removing two hydrogen atoms while holding the other counts fixed increases DBE by 1.

Common confusion

Apply the neutral-formula calculation only after establishing the ion-to-neutral relationship. Inserting the extra proton of [M+H]+ would change this example's result to 5.5, without changing the neutral molecule's unsaturation. A fractional or negative result is a reason to check assumptions, not a universal proof that a chemical species cannot exist. Radicals, charged species, and variable-valence elements need appropriate treatment. [2]

Check your understanding

Can DBE distinguish two structures sharing a formula?

No. Their atom counts give the same value; structural evidence must separate them.
