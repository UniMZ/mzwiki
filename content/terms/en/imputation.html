Definition

Imputation supplies model-based estimates for unavailable entries in a dataset. It can use a fixed rule, relationships among samples or features, or a probabilistic model. The resulting number is an estimate, not a new measurement. Proteomics benchmarks show that success depends on the downstream task; a completed matrix is not evidence of improved inference. [1]

A simple example

Hypothetical areas: a control has values [100, missing, 120], and a comparison group has mean 220. Filling with 110 produces a control mean of 110 and a comparison/control ratio of 2. Filling with 20 produces a mean of 80 and a ratio of 2.75. The measurements did not change; the missing-value assumption did. These ratios compare arithmetic means.

Common confusion

Signal re-extraction, often called gap filling, returns to acquired data and integrates a region where initial detection found no accepted peak. Statistical imputation predicts a table entry from other information. Keep their flags separate. Official xcms documentation describes re-extraction and its filled-peak flag. [2]

Single imputation can understate uncertainty if estimates are subsequently treated as observed. Multiple imputation analyzes several suitably generated datasets and combines estimates with uncertainty from both within and between imputations. It requires compatible models and pooling rules; simply repeating arbitrary random fills is insufficient. [3]

Check your understanding

May a data-dependent imputer be fitted on all data before evaluation?

No. Learn imputation inside each training fold, then apply it to held-out data. Keep related samples together at the intended independent unit; otherwise information can leak into evaluation. [4]
