A spectrum starts with an electrical measurement

A detector makes ion behavior measurable. An analyzer establishes a relationship between m/z and transmission, arrival time, or motion; the detection system supplies the electrical observations. These jobs may occur in separate components or share electrodes. This Guide follows the signal after mass analysis, building on mass analyzers without repeating their field equations.

Keep four quantities separate: ions arriving or stored, electric charge or current, recorded electronic values, and processed spectral intensity. A label such as “counts” does not establish a one-to-one molecule count. Ask what was actually measured and which transformations produced the plotted axis.

Collecting charge measures a current

A Faraday cup collects incident ions and connects to an electrometer. For positive ions, charge neutralization produces an external current. The SRS manual contrasts this direct ion-current measurement with electron-current measurement after multiplication. Neither requires assigning one discrete pulse to every arriving ion. [1]

Ideal teaching model: if N ions of the same charge-number magnitude |z| are completely collected over time t, the mean current magnitude is I = N |z| e / t. The elementary charge is exactly e = 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. [2] At 100000 singly charged ions per second, I = 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁴ A, approximately 16.02 fA. Doubling charge per ion at the same arrival rate doubles current.

This calculation assumes complete collection, no secondary-electron loss, and no interfering current. It is not a sensitivity specification. A measured current alone does not identify charge states or reveal how many original sample molecules failed to ionize, transmit, or reach the collector.

Electron multiplication increases the readable signal

In an electron multiplier, an incoming particle initiates secondary-electron emission. Electric fields accelerate electrons into successive emitting surfaces, producing a cascade. Discrete dynodes provide separate stages; a continuous-dynode channel provides repeated impacts along a surface. The multiplied particles are electrons, not newly created analyte ions. [3]

A conversion dynode can precede the multiplier. Microchannel plates use many small multiplication channels and are another impact-detection implementation. These architectures differ in collection geometry, response, and electronics; naming the detector family does not specify all its performance. Manufacturer documentation establishes these mechanisms, not a universal ranking. [4]

Counting and analog readout are different choices

Pulse counting registers accepted electronic pulses as events. A discrimination rule separates accepted pulses from smaller fluctuations. Analog readout measures the amplified output amplitude or current and digitizes it. An electron multiplier can support both modes, as demonstrated by a commercially documented dual-mode design. Therefore “electron multiplier” and “ion counter” are not interchangeable descriptions. [5]

Closely spaced arrivals can overlap or occur while counting electronics recover. Dead time can then suppress recorded events. The Agilent TOF explanation shows how timing electronics can miss a later arrival within the recovery interval; the consequences depend on the implementation and arrival distribution. Analog digitization avoids that particular one-event counting rule but still has finite range and bandwidth. It does not eliminate saturation. [6]

Constructed clipping example: an illustrative readout reports y = min(x, 100). True inputs 80 and 160 become 80 and 100, so an input ratio of 2 appears as 1.25. Rescaling those recorded values cannot reconstruct the lost upper part. Real saturation need not be an abrupt clip; this model tests the loss of information, not detector specifications.

Induced current does not require an impact

In image-current detection, moving trapped charge induces a changing signal on nearby electrodes. Orbitrap detection differentially amplifies signals on split outer electrodes, digitizes the time record, and processes it in frequency space. The ions need not strike those electrodes to contribute repeatedly. This is not pulse counting of separate arrivals. [7]

Coherent motion matters because electrical contributions add with phase. Ideal sinusoidal example: two equal contributions A sin(ωt) in phase sum to 2A sin(ωt); two equal contributions separated by π sum to zero. This illustrates cancellation, not a complete instrument-response model. A weaker oscillating signal can reflect altered coherence as well as a different population. Frequency identifies the measurement coordinate through calibration; amplitude still depends on the detection and processing model.

The stored time record is a transient. A profile mass spectrum derived from it is already a transformed representation. Keeping a profile spectrum does not necessarily preserve the underlying transient, and neither representation automatically provides an absolute ion census.

Counts have uncertainty even in an ideal experiment

For independent detected events at a constant mean rate, a Poisson model gives mean count λ and standard deviation √λ. [8] Under this model, expected counts of 25 and 100 have relative standard deviations of 20% and 10%. Four times the expected count halves relative counting uncertainty; it does not remove bias from incomplete detection.

Those are repeated-measurement properties of an ideal model, not error bars guaranteed by any intensity column. Background, changing source response, dead time, correlated arrivals, and fluctuating gain can violate its assumptions. Applying √intensity to arbitrary units or Fourier amplitudes merely because the display says “counts” is unjustified.

Read the complete response chain

To compare intensities, inspect ionization and transmission, collection or trapping, detector mode and gain, acquisition time, and processing. A larger detector response may arise without a larger concentration. Suitable standards and validation connect response to amount; see quantitative calibration. Record saturation warnings and mode changes, and examine controls before attributing an unexpected ratio entirely to sample chemistry.

Check your understanding

Does an output of 1000 mean that 1000 molecules were present?

No. Establish whether the value is accepted pulses, an analog conversion, a transformed amplitude, or a scaled quantity; then consider the upstream measurement losses.

Does stronger electronic amplification create more ion observations?

No. It changes the readable electrical response. Independent ion observations, detection efficiency, noise, and saturation remain separate questions.
