The central idea

Mass spectrometry (MS) studies matter by producing gas-phase ions and measuring signals associated with their mass-to-charge ratios, written m/z. A neutral molecule must become an ion before it can be manipulated by electric fields. The detected ion is not necessarily the original molecule: it may have gained a proton, acquired an adduct, or broken into fragments. IUPAC provides the terminology used throughout this wiki. [1]

A mass spectrum places m/z on the horizontal axis and signal intensity on the vertical axis. Each peak represents an ion population within a small measurement interval. It is evidence about a sample, interpreted through an instrument and a method. [1]

The base peak is the most intense peak in a spectrum. [3] When a plot uses base-peak relative intensity, that peak is set to 100% and each other peak is scaled by relative intensity (%) = 100 × peak intensity / base-peak intensity. The denominator is the strongest peak in that same spectrum, not the sum of all peaks. [4] A 50% peak therefore has half the base peak's signal; it does not mean half the molecules or half the sample.

Follow the measurement

  1. Prepare and introduce the sample. Extraction, dilution, and chromatography determine which material reaches the source.
  2. Form ions. Ionization chemistry determines which charged species are produced.
  3. Transfer and analyze. Ion optics transmit part of that population; an analyzer distinguishes ions by m/z.
  4. Detect and process. Detector signals become peaks, chromatographic features, and candidate identifications.

In liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS), spectra are acquired repeatedly as compounds elute. A spectrum describes a moment or time interval; a chromatogram follows signal over time. An extracted ion chromatogram (XIC) follows signal in a selected m/z interval over time. [5] These views answer different questions.

These three points illustrate the two views, not validated chromatographic sampling or peak integration.

Check the denominator: the base peaks lie at m/z 200.000, 100.000, and 300.000 in the three scans; their intensities are 100, 80, and 80. If each spectrum is separately scaled to its own base peak, the 100.000 peak becomes 100 × 20 / 100 = 20%, 100 × 80 / 80 = 100%, and 100 × 40 / 80 = 50%. Those percentages use changing denominators. The XIC above uses the original 20, 80, and 40 signals instead. Keep the intensity scale with the data; relative peak heights alone cannot establish concentration.

Worked example: one compound, several peaks

Separate constructed mass example: suppose a neutral compound has a monoisotopic mass of 300.0000 Da, calculated using the most abundant isotope of each element. In positive electrospray ionization (ESI) it might produce a protonated ion, [M+H]+, near m/z 301.0073 and a sodium adduct, [M+Na]+, near 322.9892. Heavier isotopes can add further peaks. Several peaks therefore need not mean several compounds. The rounded positions use a proton mass of 1.007276467 Da and a sodium-cation mass of approximately 22.989220702 Da; both ions have charge +1. [6] [7] See the adduct calculation for the electron-mass correction.

Conversely, two compounds can produce ions at the same nominal or even exact m/z. Structural isomers can share a formula. Separation, fragmentation, standards, and other evidence help distinguish them.

Signal is not automatically concentration

Response depends on ionization efficiency, matrix effects, transmission, acquisition settings, and detector behavior. A peak twice as high does not by itself establish twice the concentration across different compounds. Quantification needs a defined response model, suitable calibration, quality controls, and often an internal standard.

Reference spectra help evaluate identity, but the ionization and fragmentation conditions must be compatible. NIST maintains distinct electron-ionization and tandem-MS reference libraries, reflecting that difference. [2]

Check your understanding

Does the tallest peak have to be the molecular ion?

No. It is the base peak—the most intense peak in that spectrum. It could be a fragment, an adduct, a contaminant, or another ion. Its intensity alone does not establish identity.

Beyond the basics

For computational work, keep the measurement layers separate: a centroid peak, a chromatographic feature, an ion assignment, and a molecular identification are different objects. Trace every processed result back to the scan, sample, and processing parameters that produced it.
