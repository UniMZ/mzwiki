Start with the ion formula

Use m/z for the conventional numerical mass-to-charge ratio; z is the charge number. State polarity separately and use the magnitude of charge when calculating the usual positive horizontal-axis values. Mass in daltons (Da) and m/z are not interchangeable labels. [1]

For a neutral monoisotopic mass M and an ion made by adding z protons, [M+zH]z+, the approximate numerical relation is m/z = (M + z × 1.007276) / z. This formula is specific to protonation. For sodium adducts, negative ions, or mixed adducts, write the actual composition first.

Worked example: recover neutral mass

A doubly protonated ion at m/z 501.007276 has neutral mass M = 2 × 501.007276 − 2 × 1.007276 = 1000.000000 Da. The singly protonated ion of the same neutral molecule would be near m/z 1001.007276. A lower m/z does not always mean a smaller molecule.

Read the isotope envelope

Atoms of one element can have different neutron counts. An organic molecule containing one 13C in place of 12C gains approximately 1.003355 Da. The spacing between corresponding peaks is therefore about 1.003355 / |z|: approximately 1.0034 at charge 1 and 0.5017 at charge 2. This is a useful charge-state clue for many carbon-containing molecules, not a universal spacing rule for every isotope pattern. NIST tabulates isotope masses and representative compositions. [2]

The monoisotopic peak uses one specified isotope for each element, conventionally the most abundant isotope in common organic MS calculations. It is not necessarily the tallest peak and may be weak for large molecules. Average mass accounts for isotope abundances; nominal mass uses integer isotope masses. Do not compare these as if they were the same quantity.

Accuracy and resolution answer different questions

Mass error in parts per million is 10⁶ × (observed − theoretical) / theoretical. Observing 500.0020 for a theoretical m/z of 500.0000 gives +4 ppm. A search window of ±5 ppm around 500 spans ±0.0025 in m/z.

At full width at half maximum (FWHM), resolving power is commonly reported as R = (m/z) / Δ(m/z). A peak at 500 with FWHM 0.010 has R = 50,000. Always state the width convention and reference m/z. Narrow peaks can still be systematically shifted by poor calibration; good accuracy does not guarantee that adjacent peaks are separated.

Check your understanding

Adjacent carbon-isotope peaks are about 0.3345 apart. What charge is plausible?

Charge magnitude 3, because 1.003355 / 3 ≈ 0.33445. Verify several peaks and the envelope before assigning charge; overlap can mislead.

Beyond the basics

Deisotoping groups isotopic peaks belonging to an ion. Charge deconvolution combines evidence across charge states to estimate neutral masses. Neither operation proves molecular identity. Preserve the original peaks and record isotope models, tolerances, and adduct assumptions so assignments can be inspected.
