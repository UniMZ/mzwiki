Start with the ion, not the neutral molecule

An ion’s mass and charge jointly determine its position on a mass spectrum. The conventional horizontal coordinate m/z is dimensionless: divide the ion mass expressed in daltons by the magnitude of its charge number. It is not the physical ratio in kg/C, and an axis labeled m/z should not be labeled Da. State positive or negative polarity separately. This follows the IUPAC convention. [1]

For the arithmetic below, let x denote that numerical coordinate, M the neutral monoisotopic mass, μ = M/Da its numerical value, and n = |z| the positive integer charge magnitude. Let h = 1.007276467, the proton mass in Da rounded from NIST CODATA. [3] We neglect binding-energy mass corrections at this teaching precision.

Proton-only ion formulas; M refers to the same neutral species
IonCoordinate xRecover neutral mass M
[M+nH]n+(μ + nh) / n(nx − nh) Da
[M−nH]n−(μ − nh) / n(nx + nh) Da

These expressions describe addition or removal of protons. A sodium adduct, a radical ion formed by electron loss, and a mixed-adduct ion require different compositions. Do not substitute the neutral hydrogen-atom mass (about 1.007825032 Da) for the proton mass. Neutral hydrogen includes an electron; the difference is about 0.0005486 Da. The atomic-mass and particle-mass tables describe different entities. [2] [3]

Worked example: one mass, several charge states

Assume a hypothetical neutral species with M = 1000.000000 Da forms only protonated ions. The predicted positions are:

IonnPredicted m/z
[M+H]+11001.007276
[M+2H]2+2501.007276
[M+3H]3+3334.340610

Use the unrounded coordinate for inverse calculations: M = (2 × 501.007276467 − 2 × 1.007276467) Da = 1000.000000 Da. A triply deprotonated ion of the same assumed neutral mass would instead occur near m/z 332.326057. Negative polarity does not require plotting a negative horizontal coordinate.

The lower-positioned ion need not be the smaller molecule. Equally, one isolated peak cannot determine both mass and charge: charge needs independent evidence, such as a resolved isotope pattern or related charge states.

Isotope spacing helps infer charge

Replacing one 12C by 13C adds 1.00335483507 Da. Thus, for corresponding carbon-isotope peaks of the same ion, Δx = 1.00335483507 / n. NIST supplies the isotope masses. [4] At n = 1, 2, and 3, the spacings are about 1.003355, 0.501677, and 0.334452, respectively. Compare several intervals before assigning charge; overlapping envelopes or missing peaks can mimic another spacing.

An isotopologue differs in isotope composition. The monoisotopic mass is the exact mass calculated using the most abundant isotope of each element. For another specified isotopic composition, calculate that isotopologue’s exact mass. [1] The average mass weights isotope compositions by abundance. The monoisotopic peak is not necessarily the base peak (the most intense peak). Do not use an average mass as a monoisotopic search target.

Build an isotope distribution from probabilities

Here is an intentionally simplified model: a molecule has N carbon atoms, each independently 13C with fixed probability p = 0.0107 and otherwise 12C. Hold all other elements at fixed isotopes and ignore radioactive carbon. The value of p is a representative NIST composition, not a universal constant for every sample. [4]

If K is the number of 13C atoms, its binomial probability is P(K=k) = C(N,k) pᵏ (1−p)ᴺ⁻ᵏ, for integers 0 ≤ k ≤ N. Here C(N,k) = N! / (k! (N−k)!) counts the possible placements. This is our calculation from the stated model, not an experimentally measured distribution.

Other k values hold the remaining probability. For N = 100, the k = 1 class is more abundant than k = 0: the monoisotopic peak is not the tallest in this model. Also distinguish a fraction of the total from a ratio to the monoisotopic peak. For N = 10, P(1)/P(0) = Np/(1−p) ≈ 0.108157; the M+1 signal is about 10.8157% of M, not 9.7126% of M.

The labels M, M+1, and M+2 here denote nominal isotope classes, not exact increments of 1 Da or 1 in m/z. A real molecular envelope combines isotopes of every element. For example, M+2 can include two 13C atoms replacing two 12C atoms, or one 18O replacing 16O, at different exact masses. [2] Sufficient resolving power can reveal this isotope fine structure; an unresolved nominal class can contain several contributions.

Connect the pattern to measurement quality

Charge inference depends on peak separation. A narrow-looking centroid stick has no displayed profile width, so it cannot establish resolving power. Likewise, a plausible isotope pattern does not certify the absolute mass calibration. See resolving power versus mass accuracy for definitions and the annotated simulated spectrum to combine charge, isotope ratios, and mass error.

Common misconceptions

  • “Every peak is a compound.” One compound can produce several charge states, adducts, isotopologues, and fragments.
  • “The first visible isotope peak must be monoisotopic.” The true monoisotopic peak may be below detection or obscured.
  • “M+1 directly counts carbon atoms.” The approximation assumes a carbon-dominated, interference-free pattern with suitable abundance and response assumptions; other isotopes contribute in real samples.
  • “An exact isotope match proves identity.” It supports a composition hypothesis, but structural isomers can share the same distribution.

Check your understanding

Three successive carbon-isotope peaks are about 0.3345 apart. What is plausible, and what remains unknown?

Charge magnitude 3 is plausible because 1.00335483507 / 3 ≈ 0.334452. Polarity, adduct composition, the monoisotopic assignment, and molecular identity still need evidence. The spacing alone does not supply them.

Beyond the basics

Deisotoping groups isotopic peaks of an ion. Charge deconvolution combines evidence across charge states to estimate neutral masses. Preserve the original data and document isotope models, abundance assumptions, tolerances, and adduct rules. Neither operation by itself establishes a molecular identity.
