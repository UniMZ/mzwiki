Choose a representation before choosing an algorithm

A profile spectrum preserves sampled intensity across spectral peaks. A centroid spectrum stores one coordinate and intensity for each reported peak. PSI-MS distinguishes these representations from the peak-picking operation that converts between them. [1] Neither representation specifies molecular identity. This Guide examines what processing keeps, estimates, and discards before feature finding and identification.

“Raw” is contextual: vendor data may already include digitization, calibration, averaging, or Fourier processing. Profile does not necessarily mean an untouched detector waveform. Establish the earliest available measurement, then retain its relationship to every derived file.

The sampled shape contains additional evidence

Profile data can reveal shoulders, asymmetry, baseline behavior, clipping, and peak width. A peak list ordinarily omits that sampled shape, although a format or algorithm may separately retain width or boundary estimates. Plotting sticks as connected lines does not recover discarded measurements.

Coordinate spacing need not be uniform. A simple sum of sampled intensities is not automatically an integral; integration must account for coordinate intervals. Spectral integration across m/z is also different from chromatographic integration across time. Both may produce numbers called “area,” so report the axis, boundaries, baseline rule, and units.

A centroid result depends on its estimator

Despite the name, centroiding is not universally an intensity-weighted mean. A method can estimate a peak maximum or fit a shape. OpenMS PeakPickerHiRes, for example, reconstructs a peak with a cubic spline and reports the spline maximum's coordinate and intensity. Its assumptions suit particular high-resolution data; this example does not endorse it for every acquisition. [2]

Constructed weighted-mean example: take coordinates [499.98, 499.99, 500.00, 500.01, 500.02] and baseline-subtracted intensities [0, 2, 7, 3, 0]. Under the explicitly chosen rule x̄ = Σ(xᵢ yᵢ) / Σyᵢ, the coordinate is 500.0008333. The sampled maximum is instead at 500.00. This difference is an estimator choice, not a newly resolved ion.

The maximum intensity is 7, the intensity sum is 12, and trapezoidal integration over the stated bounds is 0.12 intensity-unit × m/z-coordinate-unit. These numbers summarize different quantities. PSI-MS separately names height- and area-based peak picking, without making every exporter implement an identical calculation. [1] None of these values is a time-integrated chromatographic area.

Baseline correction changes the problem being fitted

A baseline model estimates a background contribution; it does not identify which chemical species caused it. Broad real signals and overlapping tails can be mistaken for background. Record the estimation region and method, and inspect whether correction removes meaningful structure or creates negative residuals.

In the example above, adding a constant baseline of 5 gives [5, 7, 12, 8, 5]. Using those uncorrected values in the same weighted-mean formula gives approximately 500.0002703. The constant contribution pulls the estimate toward the window center. This is an arithmetic illustration with known baseline, not a recommendation to subtract a fixed value from real spectra.

A smoother trace can be a different peak

Smoothing combines neighboring samples under a model. OpenMS exposes filter width and, for Savitzky–Golay smoothing, polynomial order as explicit choices. [3] These choices should be judged against peak shape and downstream measurements, rather than visual neatness alone.

Constructed moving-average example: an isolated sequence [0, 0, 9, 0, 0] becomes [0, 3, 3, 3, 0] under a centered three-point mean with zeros beyond the boundaries. The sum remains 9 but the maximum falls from 9 to 3. This deliberately simple signal shows why preservation of one summary does not imply preservation of height or width. Neighboring output points share inputs and therefore need not have independent noise.

Define signal and noise before comparing S/N

Signal-to-noise ratio requires a defined numerator and denominator. IUPAC's spectroscopy entry starts with a power ratio and also discusses amplitude conventions. Analytical software often reports a peak-height ratio using its own noise estimator. Those values are not automatically interchangeable. [4] Thermo's ICIS documentation, for example, describes neighboring-point and iterative noise estimation for chromatograms; it is not a universal spectral-noise definition. [5]

Constructed comparison: a baseline-subtracted peak height H = 20 is assessed against residuals [−2, −1, 1, 2]. Defining RMS noise as √(Σrᵢ² / 4) gives √2.5 ≈ 1.5811 and H/RMS ≈ 12.65. Defining noise as maximum minus minimum gives 4 and H/noise = 5. The same data produce different reported ratios. Neither formula establishes the appropriate estimator for a real experiment.

Specify the noise region, exclusions, estimator, data representation, and any smoothing or baseline correction. A chemical interference can be structured signal rather than random baseline noise. A large S/N therefore does not demonstrate selectivity, molecular identity, or quantitative validity. No universal S/N threshold settles all detection or quantification tasks.

Peak picking is not feature finding

Collapsing one spectral peak into a coordinate–intensity pair does not link it across retention time, group its isotopes, or integrate its elution. These additional operations support a feature model. Similarly, estimating one location for an unresolved overlap does not separate its contributors. A deconvolution claim needs assumptions and independent evaluation beyond a smaller output file.

Audit the transformation, not just the extension

Keep the original, software version, selected MS levels, algorithm, settings, and processing order. ProteoWizard documents sequential filters and distinguishes vendor-specific from alternative peak picking. [6] Compare representative weak, strong, crowded, and isolated regions before and after processing. Check coordinates, intensity meaning, lost peaks, and any double processing of already centroided data. Successful conversion alone cannot establish scientific equivalence.

Check your understanding

Can a centroid-only file reproduce the original profile exactly?

Usually no. Many sampled shapes can lead to the same reported pair. A fitted reconstruction adds assumptions rather than recovering the discarded observations.

Does S/N = 10 mean the same thing in two programs?

Only after checking their signal definitions, noise estimators, regions, and processing. The number alone is insufficient.
