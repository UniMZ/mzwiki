Different physics, the same coordinate

A mass analyzer relates ion motion to m/z. It does not identify a compound by itself. Instruments often combine analyzers: a quadrupole may select a precursor, a collision cell may fragment it, and a time-of-flight or Orbitrap analyzer may measure products.

For each analyzer, ask three questions: what happens to the ions, what signal is measured, and how is that signal converted into m/z? Read the overview first, then work through the mechanisms before comparing performance.

Quadrupole: a stability filter

Four rods form a passage for ions. Opposite rods share a voltage; the two rod pairs receive opposite combinations of a direct-current (DC) voltage and an alternating radio-frequency (RF) voltage. For a chosen setting, a band of m/z values has stable transverse motion in both directions and can pass through. Other trajectories grow too large to be transmitted. This is a test of motion in an electric field, not a chemical recognition step. [4]

In a conventional mass scan at a fixed RF frequency, the instrument changes RF and DC amplitudes together so that the transmitted band moves across the chosen range. Recording detector signal against that changing setting builds a spectrum. Holding selected settings instead enables selected-ion monitoring. [5] A fixed isolation window can pass several different ions; selecting a nominal m/z does not establish molecular identity.

Do not infer the function from four rods alone. An RF-only quadrupole can guide a broad band above a low-m/z cutoff without the narrow RF/DC filtering described here. Such ion guides and collision-cell devices have different jobs. [6]

Time of flight: turn arrival time into m/z

A TOF experiment launches an ion packet and records when ions arrive at a detector. Under controlled starting conditions, lower-m/z ions arrive earlier. A pulse defines the time origin; this can come from pulsed ion formation or pulsed extraction of an existing ion beam. [7]

Ideal teaching model: assume negligible initial kinetic energy, an acceleration potential difference of magnitude V, a field-free path of length L, and nonrelativistic motion without collisions. Let M be the physical ion mass, n = |z| the positive charge-number magnitude, e the elementary charge, and u the dalton expressed as a physical mass. For the numerical spectral coordinate x = M / (u n), energy conservation gives M v² / 2 = n e V. Substituting τ = L / v yields τ = L √(u x / (2 e V)), so x ∝ τ². Ions accelerated through the same V receive the same energy per charge, not necessarily the same total energy.

Constructed ratio example: if x = 100 has a corrected flight time τ = 10 microseconds, the same ideal setup gives 20 microseconds at x = 400 and 30 microseconds at x = 900. Four times the m/z gives twice the flight time. In a calibrated instrument, the measured time may include an offset: use τ = t − t₀, not t blindly.

Real packets have spreads in position, energy, and launch time. A reflectron is an electrostatic ion mirror: at the same m/z, higher-energy ions penetrate farther and spend longer in the mirror, which can compensate for some flight-time spread. [7] Actual ion optics and calibration are more elaborate than the straight-path teaching equation; it is not a universal instrument calibration formula.

RF ion traps: store, select, activate, and eject

An RF ion trap confines ions for a controlled period. A linear quadrupole trap can analyze them by changing trapping and excitation conditions so that different m/z groups leave for detection in sequence. It can also isolate ions, activate them, keep selected products, and repeat the sequence for MSn. These operations are demonstrated in the primary linear-trap study. [8]

Storage makes a sequence of experiments possible within one device, but stored ions are not independent of one another. Their mutual electrical repulsion is called space charge; it changes motion when the population becomes too dense. [9] Ion population, scan conditions, and the chosen trapping/activation settings therefore belong in a method description.

Here “RF ion trap” means the quadrupole-trap family. Orbitrap and FT-ICR instruments also trap ions, but the frequency-based detection described below differs from mass-selective ejection.

Frequency analysis: listen to repeated ion motion

Moving ion packets induce small electrical signals in nearby electrodes. An image-current measurement records this signal while ions remain in the analyzer, rather than requiring each ion to strike a detector. The resulting time record is a transient. Fourier transformation separates its frequency components, then a calibration relation maps frequency to m/z. This workflow underlies both Orbitrap and FT-ICR measurements. [10] [11]

Orbitrap: measure axial oscillation

Ions orbit a central electrode while oscillating along its axis in an electrostatic field. The frequency used for mass analysis is the axial oscillation frequency, not simply the rate of travel around the electrode. In the ideal field, f = C / √x, where x is the numerical m/z and C is fixed by the analyzer field and geometry. [2]

Constructed example: choose C so that x = 200 gives f = 100 kHz. At x = 800, f is 50 kHz. Thus x = (C / f)²: increasing m/z decreases frequency. These chosen values teach the relationship and are not specifications for an instrument.

FT-ICR: measure motion in a magnetic field

A magnetic field bends an ion's motion. For ideal, nonrelativistic free cyclotron motion perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of magnitude B, equating magnetic and centripetal forces gives n e v B = M v² / r. With f_c = v / (2πr) and the definitions above, f_c = e B / (2π u x). At fixed B, the unperturbed cyclotron frequency is proportional to 1 / x.

In FT-ICR, excitation organizes ion motion for a measurable time-domain signal, followed by Fourier analysis. [11] A real ICR cell also uses electric trapping fields; the commonly detected reduced cyclotron frequency differs from the ideal free frequency. Practical calibration must account for the actual measurement conditions. [12]

Separate constructed example: choose B so the ideal fc is 100 kHz at x = 200. At x = 800 it is 25 kHz. The Orbitrap example above drops by a factor of two; this ideal ICR example drops by a factor of four. The different exponents matter when interpreting performance across a mass range.

Observation time is one part of acquisition time

Close frequencies become easier to distinguish when a useful, coherent signal is observed for longer. A simple mathematical scale is the unpadded discrete Fourier transform grid: for N samples at sampling frequency fs, T = N / f_s and Δf_grid = f_s / N = 1 / T. A 0.10-second record has 10 Hz grid spacing; a 0.20-second record has 5 Hz. This is grid spacing, not a universal FWHM or a guaranteed two-peak separation.

Adding zeros to the same recorded sequence makes the computed frequency grid denser without observing ions for longer. Signal decay, noise, and the processing method still affect the peak you obtain. Actual Fourier processing can change reported resolving power at the same recorded duration; a primary instrument study demonstrates the importance of phase information. [13]

Transient duration also differs from ion accumulation time and the complete acquisition cycle. Loading, isolation, activation, readout, and other events can contribute; some instruments overlap operations. [13] Check the actual cycle timing when deciding how often a chromatographic peak is sampled.

Follow the ion through a hybrid instrument

Read a hybrid instrument description as a sequence of jobs: make ions → select or transmit ions → optionally activate them → measure the resulting ions. The quadrupole in a Q-TOF or quadrupole–Orbitrap arrangement can select an incoming band; the later analyzer measures the ions that emerge from the experiment. “High-resolution MS/MS” therefore does not, by itself, specify a narrow precursor isolation window. See Fragmentation & tandem MS for the selection-to-product sequence and Mass analyzer for the terminology.

Worked example: a resolution claim

A specification states R = 50,000 at m/z 500 using full width at half maximum (FWHM). Let x be the numerical m/z coordinate and w the profile peak width on that same coordinate scale, measured between the two half-height crossings after baseline subtraction. Then R = x / w and w = 500 / 50,000 = 0.010. R is dimensionless. The width here is a difference in m/z coordinates, not a neutral mass in Da.

FWHM and a two-peak valley criterion are different measurement conventions; a resolution number without its convention and reference m/z is incomplete. [1] Two ions separated by 0.004 near 500 may not be adequately distinguished at this width. The ratio 500 / 0.004 = 125,000 merely sets a width scale equal to their separation; it does not guarantee baseline separation. Peak shape, relative intensity, and signal quality matter.

Resolving power, mass accuracy, and precision

Resolving power concerns the width or separation of peaks. Mass accuracy concerns closeness to a reference value; signed mass error measures a particular deviation. Precision concerns agreement among repeated measurements. Calibration and interference affect the reliability of measured positions, even when peaks are narrow. Official instrument guidance discusses why accuracy must be evaluated under defined conditions. [3]

Define xobs as an observed coordinate and xref as the reference for the same ion composition, charge, and isotope. Then error (ppm) = 10⁶ × (x_obs − x_ref) / x_ref. A positive value means the measured position is too high. Comparing a protonated ion to a neutral mass or the wrong isotope gives a meaningless accuracy assessment.

A matching window is not a peak width

For a chosen tolerance t in ppm, a symmetric search window around xref has half-width δ = x_ref × t × 10⁻⁶. At 500, ±5 ppm means ±0.0025, or 499.9975–500.0025. At 1000, the same tolerance means ±0.0050. A fixed coordinate window and a fixed ppm window therefore behave differently across the spectrum.

A tolerance is an analysis decision, not a statement that every accepted match is correct. Do not infer it from the number of displayed decimal places. Use calibration evidence, suitable controls, and the distribution of errors under the relevant method. A centroid list alone does not retain the profile width needed for a FWHM measurement. Performance is a set of tradeoffs

Longer transients can improve frequency discrimination in Fourier-transform measurements, but take time. In an LC experiment, the instrument must still sample the chromatographic peak often enough. Higher nominal resolving power is not automatically the best acquisition choice.

Mass accuracy concerns deviation from a reference value. Resolving power concerns peak separation. Sensitivity concerns response to analyte amount under a defined method. Dynamic range concerns the usable signal span. Compare them under stated conditions, not as a single ranking of instruments.

Check your understanding

Does high resolving power eliminate a co-isolated precursor?

No. Precursors can enter the same isolation window before high-resolution product measurement. Better product separation can help interpretation, but does not undo the mixed fragmentation event.

Does a TOF ion with twice the mass always arrive later?

No. Compare m/z, not mass alone. In the ideal model, an ion of mass 800 u and charge magnitude 2 has the same x = 400 as an ion of mass 400 u and charge magnitude 1, so both have the same flight time under the stated conditions.

Does doubling the displayed Fourier grid density double the observation time?

No. Zero filling can increase the number of plotted frequency points from the same recorded signal. A longer physical transient requires additional measured samples over a longer time.

Beyond the basics

Centroiding, Fourier processing, calibration, and peak fitting can change the reported peak list. For algorithm evaluation, retain the acquisition settings and distinguish profile data from centroid data. A benchmark on one analyzer and processing pipeline need not transfer unchanged to another.
