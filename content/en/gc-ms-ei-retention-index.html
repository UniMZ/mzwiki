Two kinds of evidence from one separation

Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) combines separation in time with measurement of ions. In a conventional GC-EI experiment, compounds travel through the column as neutral molecules before entering the ion source. The method suits material that can enter the gas phase and survive the analytical conditions. A derivatization step can make an otherwise difficult analyte suitable, but the resulting spectrum and retention behavior belong to the derivative actually measured.

This Guide asks two complementary questions: what ion pattern does a component produce, and where does it elute relative to reference compounds? Neither a familiar fragment nor a nearby retention value alone establishes identity.

What electron ionization changes

Electron ionization (EI) uses electrons to ionize gas-phase species. For a neutral closed-shell molecule, a representative reaction is M + e− → M+• + 2e−. The molecular ion has lost an electron; it is not the protonated molecule [M+H]+. The dot marks an unpaired electron. [1] Fragmentation can then distribute signal among smaller ions, and the molecular ion may be weak or absent.

Many reference EI spectra use a 70 eV electron-energy convention; NIST's contribution guidance explicitly identifies that condition. [2] This is the incoming electron energy, not a statement that every molecule retains 70 eV of internal energy. Reproducible conditions make pattern comparisons useful, but source conditions, background, and instrument response still deserve attention.

Read the pattern before naming the molecule

Constructed nominal-mass example: a positive-ion EI spectrum has relative intensities 100 at m/z 43, 40 at m/z 91, and 15 at m/z 120. The base peak is 43 because it is strongest. If independent evidence supports the 120 peak as a singly charged molecular radical cation, its nominal molecular mass is consistent with 120 Da. The strongest fragment is not automatically the molecular ion.

The difference 120 − 91 = 29 is an arithmetic observation. Even with singly charged assignments, one full-scan EI spectrum does not prove that the 91 ion formed directly from the 120 ion, nor identify a unique neutral loss or structure. Inspect the whole pattern, isotope information, competing candidates, and whether the signals share an elution profile.

Put retention on a reference scale

A retention index (RI) places an analyte between reference compounds. In the usual n-alkane scale, the alkane with n carbon atoms is assigned 100n. The calculation depends on the temperature regime. Isothermal Kováts indices use logarithms of adjusted retention times; temperature-programmed linear indices use direct time differences. [3] These are distinct conventions, not interchangeable formulas.

For adjacent bracketing n-alkanes in a programmed run, write I = 100[n + (tR,x − tR,n)/(tR,n+1 − tR,n)]. Use times measured under the same method, in consistent units. NIST records the index type together with column and temperature information. [4]

Worked example: calculate and challenge an index

Constructed temperature-programmed example: n-decane (C10) elutes at 5 min, n-undecane (C11) at 7 min, and analyte X at 6 min. Thus I = 100[10 + (6 − 5)/(7 − 5)] = 1050. X is halfway between these reference times. RI is dimensionless; 1050 does not mean X contains 10.5 carbon atoms.

For an isothermal run, let t′R = tR − tM, where tM is the hold-up time. The corresponding formula is I = 100[n + log(t′R,x/t′R,n)/log(t′R,n+1/t′R,n)]. Positive adjusted times are required. If constructed adjusted times are 1, 2, and 4 min for C10, X, and C11, respectively, the index is again 1050 because log(2)/log(4) = 0.5. Incorrectly applying linear interpolation to those values gives about 1033.3. This numerical difference comes from changing the calculation, not the chemical identity.

Use agreement without overstating it

Suppose two library candidates have similar EI patterns, but their method-compatible reference indices are 1048 and 1130. Against the constructed measurement of 1050, the first has better retention agreement. That supports prioritizing it for further checking; it does not establish an automatic acceptance threshold. Index uncertainty, stationary phase, temperature program, and reference variability matter. An index from an incompatible method cannot safely reject a candidate.

Coelution can combine fragments from different compounds. NIST's AMDIS discussion separates reconstruction of component spectra from subsequent library identification. [5] Explore that distinction in mixed spectra and deconvolution. A better library score after processing still needs inspection of the underlying evidence.

Check your understanding

Does RI 1050 reveal X's carbon count?

No. It locates X relative to the reference alkane scale under the stated conditions, not within a molecular formula.

Can the 70 eV setting guarantee a visible molecular ion?

No. Ion formation and subsequent fragmentation determine the observed pattern; the molecular ion can remain weak or absent.

What should accompany an EI library candidate?

Keep the query spectrum, processing history, source conditions, candidate alternatives, RI convention, reference-alkane times, chromatographic method, and remaining uncertainty. A matched authentic standard can provide additional evidence.
