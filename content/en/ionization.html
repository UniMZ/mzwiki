The source shapes what you see

An ion source converts sample material into gas-phase ions. Its chemistry affects charge states, adducts, fragmentation, and which compounds are detectable. “Soft” ionization means relatively limited fragmentation under suitable conditions; it does not guarantee an intact molecular ion for every compound.

Four useful starting points

MethodHow ions formTypical fit and limitation
Electrospray (ESI)A charged liquid spray produces droplets that shrink and yield gas-phase ions.LC-compatible; useful for polar or ionizable molecules, peptides, and proteins. Salts and coeluting material can suppress signal.
MALDIA laser irradiates analyte in a matrix that assists desorption and ionization.Useful for biomolecules, polymers, and imaging. Matrix peaks can complicate low-mass regions; sample preparation influences results.
Electron ionization (EI)Energetic electrons ionize gas-phase molecules, often producing radical cations and fragments.Common with GC for sufficiently volatile, thermally stable compounds. The molecular ion may be weak or absent.
APCIA vaporized sample undergoes atmospheric-pressure reagent-ion chemistry, commonly initiated by a corona discharge.Useful for some less polar LC analytes that tolerate vaporization. Suitability depends on gas-phase chemistry and thermal stability.

These are starting points, not exclusive application boundaries. Source design, solvent, additives, polarity, and settings all matter. Consult the instrument method and record them when comparing spectra.

Why ESI can reveal large molecules

ESI can produce multiply charged ions. This brings large molecules into accessible m/z ranges and produces charge-state distributions rather than one peak per molecule. Fenn and colleagues established ESI as a practical route to large biomolecular ions. [1] A protein envelope must therefore be interpreted with a charge model; its most intense peak is not its neutral mass.

Worked example: choose the question first

You want to analyze a peptide mixture in aqueous organic solvent. LC–ESI–MS is a plausible starting point because separation and continuous ion production work together. A volatile aroma compound may instead suit GC–EI–MS, where reproducible fragmentation can support reference-library matching. These choices still require standards and method validation for the actual sample.

EI reference spectra and ESI tandem spectra are not interchangeable. The starting ions and energy deposition differ; use a compatible library and retain the ionization metadata. NIST provides separate library families. [3]

Before concluding that a compound is absent

Inspect blanks and standards. Consider incorrect polarity, an unexpected adduct, ion suppression, poor extraction, in-source fragmentation, and an unsuitable mass range. A dilution series can reveal non-proportional response, but it does not isolate every cause. Change one controlled variable at a time.

Check your understanding

Can a soft source produce fragments?

Yes. Source and transfer conditions can activate ions. A fragment generated before precursor selection may be mistaken for a separate analyte unless retention behavior and related ions are examined.

Beyond the basics

Computational adduct grouping should combine expected mass differences with retention time and peak-shape evidence. Shared elution alone is insufficient in a crowded mixture. For vocabulary and source definitions, use the IUPAC recommendations. [2]
