Add a gas-phase separation coordinate

Ion mobility separates gas-phase ions according to their motion through a gas under an electric field. Coupling it to MS adds information beyond m/z: ions with the same measured mass-to-charge ratio can have different mobilities. That extra coordinate can help separate interferences or constrain candidates, but it is not a direct photograph of a molecule. Distinguish the instrument's observed coordinate, a derived mobility, and a derived collision cross section. [1]

This Guide introduces how to read those quantities and compare them responsibly. The worked values are deliberately constructed for arithmetic, not specifications or performance claims for a commercial instrument.

From motion to mobility

For an ideal uniform-field drift region with negligible bulk gas flow along the drift direction, drift velocity vd = KE. Here K is mobility and E is electric-field strength. If the effective drift length is L and the time within that region is tD, K = L/(EtD). K can be expressed in m2 V−1 s−1 or cm2 V−1 s−1. These equations require the appropriate drift time, not every recorded elapsed time.

Reduced mobility K0 corrects for gas number density using a stated reference: K0 = K(N/N0). Record the reference convention. Density normalization does not erase changes in gas composition, temperature-dependent collision behavior, or reduced field E/N. Reporting recommendations treat these as part of the measurement's definition. [1]

Different instruments observe different coordinates

  • Drift-tube IM: an electric field drives ions along a gas-filled region. Stepped-field measurements can separate the drift contribution from time outside that region; calibrated single-field workflows also exist. [2]
  • Traveling-wave IM: moving electric potential waves transport ions. The relationship between arrival time and mobility is not the simple uniform-field formula above; wave conditions and an appropriate conversion procedure matter. [3]
  • Trapped IM: an electric field counteracts gas flow, and ions are released as the field is scanned. The release coordinate is converted to mobility using the instrument model and calibration. [4]
  • Differential mobility methods: separation uses differences in mobility at high and low fields. A compensation-voltage setting is not itself a conventional low-field CCS value. [5]

Consequently, an axis labeled “mobility” may represent arrival time, inverse reduced mobility, or another coordinate. Read its units and metadata before comparing peaks across instruments. Do not apply a drift-tube equation to a trapped-ion release time or a traveling-wave transit time without the required model.

Worked example: subtract the non-drift time

Assume an ideal uniform field, L = 0.100 m, E = 10,000 V/m, and a measured arrival time of 52.0 ms. An independently established 2.0 ms belongs to transport outside the drift region. Therefore tD = 50.0 ms = 0.0500 s, and K = 0.100/(10,000 × 0.0500) = 0.000200 m2 V−1 s−1, or 2.00 cm2 V−1 s−1.

Using 52.0 ms directly instead gives 1.923 cm2 V−1 s−1, approximately 3.85% lower. The arithmetic demonstrates why the time definition matters. It does not establish a universal correction of 2 ms: real corrections depend on instrument geometry and settings. If N equals the explicitly chosen N0, K0 equals K in this example; otherwise apply the density factor.

What collision cross section means

A mobility-derived collision cross section (CCS, often Ω) is an effective ion–gas collision quantity derived through a physical model, commonly reported in Å2. It is sensitive to ion structure and ion–gas interactions, rather than simply the geometric silhouette of a neutral molecule. Under matching low-field conditions, the conversion depends on mobility, charge, temperature, gas density, and the ion–gas reduced mass. [1]

Specify which ion was measured: [M+H]+, [M+Na]+, and [M−H]− are different species. Their CCS values are not interchangeable entries for “M.” A primary small-molecule drift-tube study measured multiple ion forms in nitrogen and used CCS as an additional candidate constraint. [6] If one ion has multiple mobility peaks, retain that distribution instead of silently replacing it with one unexplained average.

Compare like with like

Before interpreting a CCS difference, check ion composition and charge, bath gas, temperature, field regime, measurement method, and calibration provenance. For calibrated results, record calibrant identities, reference values, fitting procedure, and the range validated. Do not assume a calibration performs equally well far outside that range. Cross-laboratory drift-tube work demonstrates the value of standardized measurements; its measured performance does not become an accuracy guarantee for unrelated workflows. [2]

Constructed comparison: a query gives 202 Å2 and a candidate reference gives 200 Å2, a +1.0% difference relative to the reference. Whether that is acceptable depends on measurement uncertainty, reference uncertainty, bias, and a validated matching rule. Neither “within 2%” nor “within 1%” is a universal identification criterion. A helium reference and a nitrogen query should not be treated as directly interchangeable merely because both use Å2.

Separation is not complete identification

Some isomers separate; others overlap at the available resolving power. A single mobility peak does not prove sample purity. Multiple peaks can reflect distinct compounds, conformers, or different protonation-site populations, requiring additional evidence to distinguish those explanations. Source and transfer conditions can also change the ions entering the mobility region. Gas-phase structure therefore needs its own interpretation before being used to infer solution structure. [1]

Use mobility alongside accurate mass, isotopes, retention, and diagnostic fragmentation. Agreement can narrow a candidate set; disagreement should trigger a check of ion identity and measurement comparability before a chemical conclusion.

Check your understanding

Two ions have equal CCS values. Must they be the same structure?

No. Different structures can have overlapping CCS values, especially within finite uncertainty. A matching CCS is supporting evidence, not a unique structural identifier.

Beyond the basics

Connect the comparison back to annotation confidence and lipid structural specificity. Preserve raw mobility coordinates and processing provenance so a future reader can revisit the conversion and the claim.
