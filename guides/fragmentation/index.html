Guide 05 · Methods

Fragmentation & tandem MS

Trace precursor isolation, compare activation methods, and interpret charge-aware product and neutral-loss evidence.

FOUNDATIONAL GUIDE · OCT 2026
ENZH
Before you begin: How molecules become ions, Mass analyzers & resolution

Select → activate → measure

Tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) adds a relationship: which ions can arise from an earlier ion population? A common product-ion experiment has three jobs. Instruments can perform them in different regions or at different times in a trap; MS/MS does not require two separate instruments. Other MS/MS scan types ask different questions. [1]

  1. Isolate: transmit or retain a chosen m/z window. A survey scan can guide the choice, or the method can use a predefined target or window. A precursor ion is the starting ion for the reaction being considered.
  2. Activate or react: collisions, electrons, photons, or other interactions can open reaction pathways. Only some ions may dissociate; some products can react again.
  3. Measure: a mass analyzer distinguishes the resulting ions by m/z. The recorded spectrum may include fragment ions and surviving precursor ions. An unreacted precursor is not a newly formed product.

A product ion is a reaction outcome, a broader category than a fragment: a reaction can change charge without breaking the molecular skeleton. “Precursor” and “product” describe roles in a specified step. A product selected for another reaction becomes that step's precursor. [1]

What the product spectrum leaves out

Ordinary product-ion mass analysis records charged species within its usable range. A neutral coproduct has no peak in that ion spectrum. Peak intensity also depends on competing pathways, ion transmission, measurement settings, and response. A missing peak does not establish that its proposed chemical pathway is impossible. Keep the ionization method, polarity, precursor assignment, isolation width, activation settings, and product measurement range with the data.

Activation changes the evidence

Collision-based methods

In collision-induced dissociation (CID), ion–gas collisions can convert some relative translational energy into internal excitation, followed by dissociation. Resonant excitation of stored ions and acceleration through a gas-filled collision region deliver energy differently. [1] Higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) is a beam-type collisional implementation; it belongs within collision-based fragmentation. A primary instrument study explicitly contrasts beam-type HCD with resonant-excitation collisional activation. [4]

Ion composition, charge, gas, energy, and reaction time affect which pathways compete. Increasing a collision-energy setting can change both the extent of fragmentation and secondary fragmentation; it does not simply reveal more copies of one fixed spectrum. Record the actual method and units. Equal displayed energy numbers, especially normalized settings, need not mean equivalent activation across instruments.

Electron-based methods: a useful peptide example

In electron capture dissociation (ECD), a multiply charged positive ion captures an electron. In conventional positive-ion electron transfer dissociation (ETD), a reagent anion transfers an electron to the cation. Foundational peptide experiments showed backbone fragmentation complementary to collisional methods and retention of some labile modifications. These are useful capabilities, not guarantees for every sequence or modification. [5] [2]

The positive charge decreases by one upon electron capture or transfer, without removing a proton. For example, the immediate charge-reduced species from [M+3H]3+ is [M+3H]2+•, not [M+2H]2+. The dot denotes an unpaired electron; this is composition and charge bookkeeping, not an exact-mass calculation. Include the incoming electron or reagent ion when balancing the whole reaction.

Electron transfer can also leave a charge-reduced species without separated fragments, often called electron transfer without dissociation (ETnoD). Sequence, charge state, conformation, and reaction conditions matter; supplemental activation can help in some experiments. There is no universal “ETD only works above 2+” rule: a primary supplemental-activation study examined doubly protonated peptides. [6] This section concerns common ECD/ETD peptide applications, not every electron-driven reaction or every small molecule. Electron ionization (EI), introduced in the ionization Guide, is a different experiment.

A first look at peptide ions

Peptides make a useful worked language for backbone cleavage. The letter records a cleavage type and the retained terminus; the subscript counts residues from that terminus. Charge and radical state are additional information. These peptide labels are not a universal naming system for small-molecule fragments. [7]

For an abstract four-residue peptide A–G–S–K, b2 refers to an N-terminal two-residue product and y2 to a C-terminal two-residue product. The subscript 2 is a residue count, not a charge. A superscript 2+ would state charge. This naming example predicts neither peak presence nor intensity. Spectra can contain other ion series, neutral losses, internal fragments, and multiple charge states. [7]

A residue gap needs a charge assumption

Constructed arithmetic: the monoisotopic alanine residue increment C3H5NO is 3 × 12 + 5 × 1.00782503223 + 14.00307400443 + 15.99491461957 = 71.03711378515 Da, using NIST isotope masses as fixed central-value teaching constants, without uncertainty propagation. [9] [10] [11] [12] This is the residue increment within a peptide, not the mass of free alanine.

For consecutive assigned ions in one terminal series, with unchanged ion chemistry and no additional loss, that increment produces an m/z gap of 71.0371 at charge +1, or 35.5186 when both ions have charge +2. A matching gap alone proves neither alanine's position nor a complete sequence. A wrong series assignment, a skipped residue, a modification, or overlapping ions can give a misleading interpretation.

Balance composition and charge before subtracting peaks

For a closed dissociation channel, account for all atoms, charges, and any neutral products. For an ion–ion or electron reaction, include the other reactants too. Fragmentation can rearrange atoms; a plausible product formula need not correspond to cutting one bond in a drawing. Conventional exact-mass bookkeeping sums isotope masses and electron/proton corrections, neglecting the much smaller chemical binding-energy contribution at the precision used here.

Constructed ion-mass example: suppose an isolated 1000.0000 Da ion with charge +2 splits into 600.0000 Da and 400.0000 Da ions, each +1, with no neutral or exchanged species. These are abstract bookkeeping inputs, not proposed formulas or measured peaks. Charge balances as +2 = +1 + +1, and ion masses as 1000 = 600 + 400. Yet the precursor is at m/z 500 and the products at 600 and 400. A product can have higher m/z than its precursor because its charge is lower.

Let x be the numerical m/z coordinate and n = |z|. The ion mass in Da is numerically n × x. Subtracting two x values directly gives a mass difference only after the charge relationship and reaction chemistry are specified. See Charge state and m/z.

Worked example: neutral loss

A neutral loss removes an uncharged species during ion dissociation. In a simple channel Pz → Qz + N, the ionic product retains the precursor's charge and N is neutral. [8] Assuming no other exchanged or lost species and consistent isotope assignments, m(N) / Da = n × (x_P − x_Q). The mass of the neutral loss and the m/z shift have different roles.

Constructed water-loss calculations, not observations: choose 1H216O. Its mass is 2 × 1.00782503223 + 15.99491461957 = 18.01056468403 Da. [9] [10] Treat the following starting coordinates as exact model inputs; calculate before rounding.

Do not call every 18-unit gap “water.” Even a compatible accurate loss mass identifies neither a unique structure nor the atom-by-atom mechanism; the neutral itself was not measured in this product-ion spectrum.

Use a pattern, not one diagnostic-looking peak

One window can produce a mixed spectrum

Isolation can include several ionic forms, such as isotope peaks or different adduct and charge states. Ions from different analytes can also be coisolated and fragmented together; their combined MS/MS signals are commonly called a chimeric spectrum. A primary peptide study demonstrated this identification problem. An ordinary isotope envelope alone does not imply multiple analytes, and the study's measured prevalence is specific to its samples and settings. [13]

Ideal window exercise: assume a perfectly rectangular, symmetric window centered at 500.2 with full width 1.0. Its bounds are 499.7–500.7, so ions at 500.2 and 500.6 both pass. Width 0.4 gives 500.0–500.4 and excludes 500.6 in this model. Real isolation profiles have instrument-dependent transmission and edges; narrowing a window does not guarantee chemical purity, especially for overlapping or isomeric ions.

High resolving power during product measurement can separate some products, but cannot by itself tell which precursor made each one. Review the precursor region and isolation settings; use chromatographic behavior, additional separation, or a more selective experiment when appropriate. Broad-window mixture handling belongs with the DIA and acquisition discussion.

A practical interpretation checklist

  1. Check the starting assignment: polarity, charge, adduct, isotope, and possible coisolation.
  2. Test several linked products: use composition and charge balance, plausible losses, and the appropriate molecular-class chemistry.
  3. Inspect contradictions: unexplained intense peaks may reflect a mixture, alternative pathways, or an incorrect candidate. Do not silently delete them to improve a score.
  4. Compare compatible reference data: match ion type and relevant activation conditions. NIST documents both EI and tandem reference collections. [3]
  5. State the remaining alternatives: combine fragmentation with retention evidence, standards, and other measurements where available. A shared fragment or loss supports a hypothesis; it rarely singles out one structure.

Check your understanding

A +2 precursor and a +2 product differ by 9.0053 in m/z. What does that suggest?

Under the simple unchanged-charge loss model, the neutral mass difference is about 18.0106 Da, compatible with water. It is not proof of water's origin, a unique mechanism, or molecular identity.

Must every product have lower m/z than the precursor?

No. Charge can change or be divided among products. Compare ion masses and balance the full reaction rather than comparing m/z alone.

Does one matching fragment prove a candidate structure?

No. Assess the full evidence and plausible alternatives. Related compounds can share products, and a mixed spectrum can create misleading combinations.

Beyond the basics

Fragmentation trees, sequence database searches, spectral prediction, and de novo sequencing formalize these constraints. Evaluate candidate ranking separately from calibrated confidence. A model can rank a wrong candidate first when the correct structure or sequence is absent from its search space. Continue with spectrum interpretation and reliable data analysis.

References & further reading

  1. Murray et al. (2013). Definitions of terms relating to mass spectrometry (IUPAC Recommendations 2013). Pure and Applied Chemistry 85, 1515–1609.

    Terminology and definitions; DOI metadata verified via the authors' university repository.

  2. Syka et al. (2004). Peptide and protein sequence analysis by electron transfer dissociation mass spectrometry. PNAS 101, 9528–9533.

    Primary study of electron-transfer dissociation of peptide cations.

  3. NIST Standard Reference Database 1A: Mass Spectral Libraries.

    Official overview of EI and tandem mass-spectral reference collections.

  4. McAlister et al. (2011). Higher-energy collision-activated dissociation without a dedicated collision cell. Molecular & Cellular Proteomics 10, O111.009456.

    Primary instrument study; the accessible abstract distinguishes beam-type HCD from resonant-excitation collisional activation. DOI: 10.1074/mcp.O111.009456. Used for method classification, not a universal performance ranking.

  5. Zubarev, Kelleher & McLafferty (1998). Electron Capture Dissociation of Multiply Charged Protein Cations. A Nonergodic Process. Journal of the American Chemical Society 120, 3265–3266.

    Primary ECD demonstration, including electron capture, charge reduction, backbone products, and intact charge-reduced ions. The mechanistic interpretation in the title is not presented as universally settled.

  6. Swaney et al. (2007). Supplemental activation method for high-efficiency electron-transfer dissociation of doubly protonated peptide precursors. Analytical Chemistry 79, 477–485.

    Primary study of charge-reduced products and supplemental activation of doubly protonated peptides. DOI: 10.1021/ac061457f. Supports qualified ETD charge-state scope rather than an absolute exclusion of 2+ precursors.

  7. Chu et al. (2015). Proposed nomenclature for peptide ion fragmentation. International Journal of Mass Spectrometry 390, 24–27.

    Primary nomenclature proposal discussing conventional terminal-series labels and the need to specify charge, radicals, and hydrogen changes. The Guide uses familiar lower-case peptide labels with explicit charge/radical clarification; it does not declare the proposed system a universal standard.

  8. IUPAC Gold Book. Neutral loss, entry 12505.

    Definition from IUPAC Recommendations 2013, p. 1571. Supports distinguishing the uncharged species lost from the charged residual product.

  9. NIST. Atomic Weights and Isotopic Compositions for Hydrogen.

    Neutral hydrogen-1 relative atomic mass 1.00782503223, used in the water example.

  10. NIST. Atomic Weights and Isotopic Compositions for Oxygen.

    Oxygen-16 and oxygen-18 relative atomic masses 15.99491461957 and 17.99915961286, with representative isotope compositions.

  11. NIST. Atomic Weights and Isotopic Compositions for Carbon.

    Representative carbon-13 fraction 0.0107 and carbon-13 mass 13.00335483507 Da. Teaching models hold these inputs fixed; natural sample compositions vary.

  12. NIST. Atomic Weights and Isotopic Compositions for Nitrogen.

    Nitrogen-14 relative atomic mass 14.00307400443, used with carbon-12, hydrogen-1, and oxygen-16 in the constructed alanine-residue calculation.

  13. Yu, Deng & Nesvizhskii (2025). MSFragger-DDA+ enhances peptide identification sensitivity with full isolation window search. Nature Communications 16, 3329.

    Primary study of cofragmented peptides in DDA spectra, including narrow-window data. DOI: 10.1038/s41467-025-58728-z. Used to establish mixed-spectrum risk, without transferring prevalence or performance figures to other experiments.
