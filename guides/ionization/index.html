Guide 03 · Instruments

How molecules become ions

Compare ESI, MALDI, EI, and APCI by the chemistry they enable—not by a universal ranking.

FOUNDATIONAL GUIDE · OCT 2026
ENZH
Before you begin: What mass spectrometry measures, m/z, charge & isotopes

The source shapes what you see

A mass analyzer needs gas-phase ions. The ion source connects the sample to that measurement: it helps release charged species into the gas phase, and its chemistry influences which ion forms survive. One compound can therefore give several signals, while another compound in the same sample may give little signal.

Two questions make a useful starting point: How did the source produce this ion? and What composition and charge does the ion have? ESI and MALDI are often described as soft ionization methods, meaning that extensive fragmentation can be avoided. “Soft” does not promise that every analyte stays intact under every source or transfer condition. [1]

Compare sources by the sample and the question

Typical starting points; these are tendencies rather than exclusive application boundaries
MethodStarting material and processPractical consequence
Electrospray ionization (ESI)A solution forms a spray of charged droplets, from which gas-phase ions emerge.Fits continuous liquid delivery, including LC. Polar or ionizable small molecules, peptides, and proteins are common targets; multiple charge states are especially important for large molecules.
Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI)A laser illuminates analyte associated with a light-absorbing matrix, often on a prepared target.Fits spot-based analysis and spatial sampling, including imaging. Electron ionization (EI)Energetic electrons interact with gas-phase molecules.Common with GC for sufficiently volatile, thermally stable compounds. Radical molecular cations and fragments are possible; the molecular ion can be weak or absent.
Atmospheric-pressure chemical ionization (APCI)Vaporized sample encounters reagent ions, commonly generated using a corona discharge.A useful LC option for some analytes less suited to ESI, provided they tolerate vaporization and the gas-phase chemistry is favorable.

These source names describe ion production, not the mass analyzer: MALDI does not mean TOF, and ESI does not mean a particular analyzer. Source definitions follow IUPAC; the ESI and MALDI qualifications are developed below. [1]

ESI: from charged droplets to gas-phase ions

A potential difference between the liquid emitter and another electrode drives electrospray. Solvent evaporation reduces droplet size; electrostatic repulsion can then destabilize droplets and produce smaller ones. Drying gas, temperature, flow rate, solvent, and the interface influence this journey. This is more than simply adding a proton to every molecule.

Different models describe the final release of ions. In the ion evaporation model, small solvated ions leave a charged droplet surface. In the charged residue model, evaporation of the remaining solvent leaves an analyte carrying charge. In the chain ejection model, an extended polymer chain progressively emerges from the droplet. These models help explain different analytes and conditions; they should not be presented as one universal sequence that every ESI ion follows. [3]

For proteins, compact structures under native-like conditions are commonly discussed using charged-residue behavior, whereas unfolded chains under denaturing conditions can follow chain ejection. Charging also depends on additives and experimental conditions, so a charge-state distribution is not by itself a unique report of solution structure. [4]

Multiple charging is a major reason ESI makes large biomolecules accessible: dividing the ion mass by a larger charge magnitude moves the signal to lower m/z. Fenn and colleagues demonstrated characteristic sequences of different charge states for large biomolecules. [2] Several such peaks may represent the same neutral species, rather than several proteins.

MALDI: the matrix is part of the experiment

In common ultraviolet MALDI, the matrix absorbs laser energy and assists release of sample material. The expanding plume contains interacting species, including neutrals and ions. Primary ion formation and subsequent reactions, such as proton or cation transfer, both matter. Mechanistic descriptions differ in how the first charges arise and how clusters participate; a single “the laser gives the molecule a proton” story misses that chemistry. [5]

Conventional MALDI peptide spectra often contain mainly singly protonated ions, which can make charge interpretation simpler. [6] This is a useful expectation, not a rule that MALDI can only make +1 ions. Other charge states and adducts can occur, and specialized atmospheric-pressure matrix-assisted laser methods have produced highly multiply charged peptide and protein ions. [7] MALDI can be operated in positive or negative polarity; the matrix, analyte, and conditions influence which ions are useful. [12]

Matrix-related ions can crowd the low-m/z region. Crystal distribution, analyte incorporation, and laser conditions can change the signal from one location to another. A matrix blank and replicate sampling are therefore important checks. Avoid assuming that MALDI is immune to contaminants or suppression simply because it can tolerate some preparations that cause difficulty in ESI. [5] [6] Studies of MALDI peptides have directly demonstrated interfering alkali-metal adduct and matrix-cluster signals. [13]

Read polarity, composition, and charge separately

Positive-ion mode records cations; negative-ion mode records anions. The sign tells you the ion's net charge, not whether it gained or lost an electron in one particular reaction. For example, a neutral molecule can gain H+ to form a positive ion or lose H+ to form a negative ion. In the notation below, M denotes the same neutral molecule, and the superscript outside the brackets gives the net charge. [1]

Common ion-form hypotheses; actual formation depends on the compound and conditions
Ion notationChargeHow to read it
[M+H]++1Protonated molecule: M plus one proton.
[M−H]−−1Deprotonated molecule: M minus one proton.
[M+Na]++1Sodium adduct: M associated with Na+.
[M+NH4]++1Ammonium adduct: M associated with NH4+.
[M+Cl]−−1Chloride adduct: M associated with Cl−.
[M+2H]2+ / [M−2H]2−+2 / −2Two protons added or removed; one M, charge magnitude 2.
[M+H+Na]2++2A mixed ion form containing one added proton and one sodium cation.
[2M+H]++1A protonated cluster containing two M units; the 2 before M does not mean charge 2.

Use “protonated molecule” for [M+H]+. A molecular radical cation M+•, commonly encountered in EI, instead has lost an electron without adding a proton. These species have different compositions and masses. Permanently charged analytes require their own charge bookkeeping; the neutral-M examples are not a universal description of all ions. [1]

Solution composition helps determine which possibilities are plausible. Sodium contamination or salts can favor sodium adducts; ammonium-containing additives can support ammonium adducts. [1] [13] An acidic or basic functional group may suggest a polarity to test, but neither its presence nor the source name guarantees the dominant ion. Keep the solvent, additives, polarity, and source settings with the spectrum.

Recover the neutral mass from an explicit ion assignment

Use x for the numerical m/z coordinate, M for the neutral monoisotopic mass, μ = M/Da, and n = |z| for the charge magnitude. The coordinate is dimensionless; polarity is stated separately. Monoisotopic mass uses the most abundant isotope of each element. First establish the monoisotopic peak and charge rather than substituting the tallest peak or an average mass. See the charge and isotope guide. [1]

For an assigned ion containing k copies of M and a net added mass of δ Da, the bookkeeping is x = (kμ + δ) / n, so M = ((nx − δ) / k) Da. A removed proton contributes a negative δ. This is our mass-balance calculation, conditional on the stated ion formula.

Approximate mass inputs in Da; retained digits support reproducible arithmetic, not nine-decimal measurement accuracy

The last three values are calculated from NIST neutral-atom isotope masses and the CODATA electron mass, about 0.000548579909 Da. Binding-energy mass corrections are neglected at this teaching precision. A neutral 1H atom has mass about 1.007825032 Da: using that in place of H+ introduces an electron-sized error. Do not subtract an electron again when you already used an ionic mass. [8] [9]

Worked examples: one assumed mass, several ion forms

These are constructed calculations, not measured peaks or a proposed compound. Assume a neutral monoisotopic mass of 300.000000 Da, the isotope choices above, and no isotope-labeling or neutral-loss complications. The table shows where each ion would occur if it formed; it does not predict that one real sample will produce them all. A. A sodium assignment changes the subtraction

For the proposed sodium adduct, M = (322.989220702 − 22.989220702) Da = 300.000000 Da. If you incorrectly call it [M+H]+, you obtain 321.981944235 Da. That 21.981944235 Da discrepancy comes from the wrong ion model, not from poor mass calibration.

The predicted singly charged sodium and protonated forms are separated by 21.981944235 in m/z. Replacing one proton with sodium in a doubly charged ion gives half that separation, 10.990972118 after rounding. Always account for charge before applying a memorized adduct difference.

B. Deprotonation requires adding the proton back

For [M−H]−, M = (298.992723533 + 1.007276467) Da = 300.000000 Da. A chloride adduct instead requires subtracting the chloride-ion mass. Both are negative ions, but their mass corrections have opposite signs.

C. Multiply by charge magnitude, then correct the composition

For [M+2H]2+, M = (2 × 151.007276467 − 2 × 1.007276467) Da = 300.000000 Da. For [2M+H]+, first subtract the proton and then divide by two: M = ((601.007276467 − 1.007276467) / 2) Da = 300.000000 Da. The first ion has two charges on one M; the second has one charge on two M units.

None of these successful inversions establishes a formula or structure. They show that an ion assignment is arithmetically consistent with an assumed neutral mass. Real assignments also need charge evidence, isotope consistency, suitable tolerances, and checks for competing explanations.

Before concluding that a compound is absent

Signal can change because of extraction, chromatography, ion production, transmission, or detection. Coeluting material and nonvolatile solutes can suppress ESI response; experiments on biological extracts have linked suppression to changes in droplet properties. This is one reason a peak's intensity is not a direct count of molecules in the original sample. [10]

  1. Check the method and the search target. Confirm polarity, mass range, scan type, plausible charge states, and candidate adducts.
  2. Check blanks and a suitable standard. Distinguish background from analyte-related signal; compare sample preparation and source conditions.
  3. Look for related evidence. Inspect isotope spacing, chromatographic peak shapes, and possible source fragments. Shared retention time helps but does not prove that peaks belong together.
  4. Test one controlled change. Dilution, cleanup, or a source-setting change may help investigate a problem, but any one result can have several explanations. Record the change and compare appropriate controls.

Common interpretation traps

  • “Positive mode means [M+H]+.” Sodium, ammonium, mixed adducts, and already charged species are alternatives.
  • “Negative mode means the molecule gained an electron.” Deprotonation and anion attachment can also produce negative ions.
  • “A lower m/z means a smaller neutral molecule.” A higher charge magnitude can move a large ion to a lower coordinate.
  • “MALDI always gives one peak and ESI always gives many charges.” Both depend on analyte and conditions; isotope peaks and adducts can add complexity even at charge 1.
  • “An adduct pair proves the compound's identity.” It supports a relationship hypothesis. Coelution, isomers, overlapping signals, and incorrect charge assignments remain possible.

Check your understanding

Two positive-ion peaks are 21.981944 apart. Can you immediately call them [M+H]+ and [M+Na]+?

No. That separation is consistent with the singly charged pair, but first establish charge and test whether their isotope patterns and chromatographic behavior support a shared origin. Unrelated peaks can have a similar difference.

Can a soft source produce a fragment that looks like another analyte?

Yes. Fragmentation can occur in the source or interface before precursor selection. Check related ions, retention behavior, and controlled source-condition changes; a fragment signal alone does not establish a second compound.

Does subtracting 1.007276467 from every peak recover neutral masses?

No. That operation fits a singly protonated monomer only. Charge, adduct composition, neutral losses, clusters, and the chosen isotope peak all matter.

Connect ion assignment to stronger evidence

Use the assigned ion form when selecting a precursor and interpreting MS/MS. Protonated and sodium-adducted forms need not fragment alike. EI reference spectra and ESI tandem spectra are not interchangeable; NIST maintains separate EI and tandem library collections. [11]

Computational grouping should retain polarity, charge, isotope assignment, adduct rules, and the original observations. Grouping related features can simplify a dataset, but it does not itself identify a structure. Continue with step-by-step spectrum interpretation to combine those hypotheses with independent evidence.

References & further reading

  1. Murray et al. (2013). Definitions of terms relating to mass spectrometry (IUPAC Recommendations 2013). Pure and Applied Chemistry 85, 1515–1609.

    Terminology and definitions; DOI metadata verified via the authors' university repository.

  2. Fenn et al. (1989). Electrospray ionization for mass spectrometry of large biomolecules. Science 246, 64–71.

    Primary study of electrospray for large biomolecules. DOI: 10.1126/science.2675315.

  3. Konermann, Ahadi, Rodriguez & Vahidi (2013). Unraveling the mechanism of electrospray ionization. Analytical Chemistry 85, 2–9.

    Mechanistic review of ion evaporation, charged residue, and chain ejection; models apply to different analyte and experimental regimes. Metadata and abstract verified through PubMed PMID 23134552.

  4. Konermann, Metwally, Duez & Peters (2019). Charging and supercharging of proteins for mass spectrometry: recent insights into the mechanisms of electrospray ionization. Analyst 144, 6157–6171.

    Review connecting native-like and denaturing protein experiments to different charging mechanisms and documenting condition dependence. Metadata and abstract verified through PubMed PMID 31560020.

  5. Knochenmuss (2006). Ion formation mechanisms in UV-MALDI. Analyst 131, 966–986.

    Critical review distinguishing primary ion generation from subsequent plume reactions and discussing competing mechanistic descriptions. Publisher metadata and abstract, and indexed author-manuscript passages, checked.

  6. Sandoval (2014). Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Analysis of Peptides. Current Protocols in Protein Science 77, 16.2.1–16.2.11.

    Peptide-analysis protocol supporting the qualified expectation of predominantly singly protonated MALDI peptide ions. Does not justify a universal charge-state or salt-tolerance claim. Metadata and abstract verified through PubMed PMID 25081740.

  7. Trimpin, Inutan, Herath & McEwen (2010). Laserspray ionization, a new atmospheric pressure MALDI method for producing highly charged gas-phase ions of peptides and proteins directly from solid solutions. Molecular & Cellular Proteomics 9, 362–367.

    Primary experimental counterexample to the claim that all matrix-assisted laser methods produce only singly charged ions. This specialized atmospheric-pressure method is not presented as conventional vacuum MALDI. Metadata and abstract verified through PubMed PMID 19955086.

  8. NIST. CODATA recommended values of the fundamental physical constants: proton mass in u and electron mass in u.

    Official constants table. Examples round the proton mass to 1.007276467 Da; u and Da name the same mass unit.

  9. NIST. Atomic Weights and Isotopic Compositions with Relative Atomic Masses.

    Isotope masses and representative compositions; sample abundances can vary.

  10. King, Bonfiglio, Fernandez-Metzler, Miller-Stein & Olah (2000). Mechanistic investigation of ionization suppression in electrospray ionization. Journal of the American Society for Mass Spectrometry 11, 942–950.

    Primary experiments on biological extracts support a role for nonvolatile solutes and changed droplet properties in ESI suppression. The article does not generalize this into the only suppression mechanism. Publisher metadata and abstract verified.

  11. NIST Standard Reference Database 1A: Mass Spectral Libraries.

    Official overview of EI and tandem mass-spectral reference collections.

  12. Knochenmuss (2016). The Coupled Chemical and Physical Dynamics Model of MALDI. Annual Review of Analytical Chemistry 9, 365–385.

    Mechanistic review covering positive and negative ionization, charge-transfer reactions, suppression, and experimental-condition dependence. Its named model is not treated as the sole universally established mechanism. Publisher abstract and metadata verified.

  13. Leite, Hajivandi, Diller & Pope (2004). Removal of sodium and potassium adducts using a matrix additive during matrix-associated laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometric analysis of peptides. Rapid Communications in Mass Spectrometry 18, 2953–2959.

    Primary peptide experiments document alkali-metal adduct patterns and matrix-cluster interference; used to qualify salt-tolerance claims. Metadata and abstract verified through PubMed PMID 15536629.
