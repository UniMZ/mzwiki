Guide 16 · Computation

Peptide identification and protein inference

Separate scores, error levels, shared-peptide mappings, protein groups, and sequence coverage using explicit teaching examples.

FOUNDATIONAL GUIDE · OCT 2026
ENZH
Before you begin: Bottom-up proteomics: from sample to protein evidence, From raw data to reliable results

Separate four questions

Peptide identification asks which candidate explains measured evidence. Protein inference asks which parent proteins are supported by the identified peptides. Confidence estimation asks how often accepted results are wrong under a stated procedure. Modification localization asks which residue carries a modification. These questions are connected, but a good answer to one does not automatically answer the others.

Start from fragment-ion evidence and the bottom-up workflow. Here, a conventional spectrum-centric database search provides the teaching model. DIA and library workflows may organize evidence differently; use the acquisition Guide for how the data were collected and inspect the actual reporting units of the analysis.

A search result depends on its candidate set

A database search enumerates candidate peptides under a sequence database, digestion rule, mass tolerance, and modification model. It compares expected fragment evidence with a measured MS/MS spectrum. SEQUEST's cross-correlation approach is one historical example of a scoring function; its numerical scale is specific to that method. A higher score is stronger agreement by that function, not a universal probability of correctness. [1]

Inspect whether the intended organism, variants, common contaminants, and relevant modifications were available as candidates. An absent correct sequence cannot win a closed search. Conversely, adding many candidates can create additional competing explanations. Fixed modifications apply a specified change wherever the rule requires it; variable modifications generate alternatives at allowed sites. Neither setting proves that the chemistry happened.

Constructed search-space exercise: a peptide has four eligible sites and each can independently be unmodified or carry one chosen modification. Without other constraints, it has 2⁴ = 16 positional states. Allowing at most one modified site leaves 1 + 4 = 5. This counts candidates, not measured forms or equally likely biological states. Software may impose additional limits, and isobaric alternatives can still need distinguishing fragments.

A PSM is a proposed relationship

A PSM connects a spectrum to a candidate peptide interpretation, including relevant modifications and charge. Retain the spectrum identifier, candidate, score, mass error, and acceptance status. A spectrum can contain coisolated species; a single top-ranked assignment need not explain every peak. Inspect competing candidates and diagnostic fragments when a consequential claim depends on one spectrum.

Search scores can be transformed into calibrated confidence estimates using additional modeling. PeptideProphet demonstrated a statistical model that distinguishes correct and incorrect assignments and estimates assignment probabilities. Such a modeled probability is a different object from an uncalibrated search score, and its validity depends on the model and data. [2]

Name the error level and denominator

Target–decoy analysis estimates incorrect assignments using deliberately constructed alternative sequences. Record decoy construction, competition, filtering, and whether discoveries are spectra, peptide sequences, modified peptide forms, or proteins. A nominal threshold and an estimated error rate are not the unknowable realized error fraction of a particular list. Read false discovery rate for the formal distinction. [3]

Constructed known-truth example: 100 accepted PSMs contain 99 correct matches to the same peptide and one incorrect match to a different peptide. The realized PSM error fraction is 1/100 = 1%. Collapsing this list to two peptide sequences gives one false sequence out of two, or 50%. This artificial example is not an estimator. It proves that counting at one level does not automatically control another.

Repeated evidence, cross-run combination, or a selected subgroup can change the relevant counting problem. A modified-peptide subset should not inherit a claimed subgroup error rate solely from the full search. Localization error and false discoveries in abundance comparisons are additional questions. Keep their procedures and thresholds distinct.

Shared peptides create genuine ambiguity

Constructed mapping example: protein A contains peptides p and q; protein B contains p and r. If only p is confidently observed, the evidence is compatible with A alone, B alone, or both. Repeating p twenty times improves evidence for that peptide but does not add a distinguishing sequence. If q is also observed and uniquely maps to A within the searched database, A receives distinguishing support; B still cannot be ruled out merely because r was not detected.

Protein inference combines such mappings with confidence and inference rules. ProteinProphet is a foundational probabilistic method that handles peptides mapping to multiple proteins and derives a supported protein list. A parsimonious explanation aims to explain evidence with fewer proteins; that preference is not direct evidence that every additional compatible protein is absent. [4]

Read a protein group before reading its name

Software can group proteins whose evidence does not distinguish them and choose a leading or representative accession for display. The mzIdentML reporting framework explicitly distinguishes these roles. A representative label can be a reporting choice, rather than proof that this exact sequence was measured independently. Preserve group membership and distinguish supporting shared peptides from distinguishing peptides. [5]

“Unique” also needs a reference: unique to a database accession, gene, or protein group are different claims. Check database release and isoform inclusion. Shared-peptide assignment for quantification is another explicit rule; assigning an intensity to one displayed protein does not retroactively make the peptide unique. The standard's links among spectra, peptide evidence, and database sequences make these decisions inspectable. [6]

Coverage measures mapped sequence positions

Sequence coverage is commonly the fraction of reference residue positions covered by accepted mapped peptides. State the reference, mapping and filtering rules. Constructed example: on a 100-residue sequence, peptides covering positions 1–10 and 6–15 cover 15 distinct residues, not 20. Coverage is 15%. Observing either peptide again does not add positions.

High coverage does not alone resolve homologs if the covered regions are shared. Low coverage is not automatically a false identification if the evidence is strong and appropriate error control is used. Coverage also does not say which distant modifications coexist on the same intact molecule. For modified sequence reporting, ProForma provides explicit representations, including ambiguity; notation records a claim, not its experimental validation. [7]

Check your understanding

Can twenty PSMs establish two proteins that share the same observed peptide?

No. Repeated measurement of the shared sequence does not distinguish its parents. Seek distinguishing sequence evidence or retain a protein-group interpretation.

What makes an identification table reviewable?

Explicit reporting units, database and search settings, confidence procedures, protein-group membership, and links back to spectra. Inspect modification positions separately in the PTM localization Guide.

References & further reading

  1. Eng et al. (2008). A Fast SEQUEST Cross Correlation Algorithm. Journal of Proteome Research 7, 4598–4602.

    Primary scoring-method paper. Supports method-specific candidate scoring; scores are not treated as universal probabilities.

  2. Keller et al. (2002). Empirical statistical model to estimate the accuracy of peptide identifications made by MS/MS and database search. Analytical Chemistry 74, 5383–5392.

    Primary PeptideProphet paper supporting statistical assignment-confidence modeling and its dependence on model and data.

  3. Elias & Gygi (2007). Target-decoy search strategy for increased confidence in large-scale protein identifications by mass spectrometry. Nature Methods 4, 207–214.

    Primary methodological discussion of target–decoy error estimation. DOI: 10.1038/nmeth1019.

  4. Nesvizhskii et al. (2003). A Statistical Model for Identifying Proteins by Tandem Mass Spectrometry. Analytical Chemistry 75, 4646–4658.

    Primary ProteinProphet paper supporting probabilistic protein inference and handling of shared peptide evidence. Constructed A/B/p/q/r examples are original.

  5. Seymour et al. (2014). A standardized framing for reporting protein identifications in mzIdentML 1.2. Proteomics 14, 2389–2399.

    Standards guidance distinguishing protein groups, leading proteins and group representatives. Does not equate a representative accession with unique experimental evidence.

  6. Vizcaíno et al. (2017). The mzIdentML Data Standard Version 1.2, Supporting Advances in Proteome Informatics. Molecular & Cellular Proteomics 16, 1275–1285.

    Standards paper supporting spectrum, peptide, database-sequence and protein-group evidence links. Version 1.2 is cited for these concepts, not asserted to be the newest release.

  7. LeDuc et al. (2022). Proteomics Standards Initiative’s ProForma 2.0: Unifying the Encoding of Proteoforms and Peptidoforms. Journal of Proteome Research 21, 1189–1195.

    Standards paper covering modified sequences, natural/chemical/artifactual changes and positional ambiguity. No claim that notation validates a chemical identity or that version 2.0 is the latest specification.
