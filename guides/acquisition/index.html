Guide 07 · Methods

Acquisition: full scan, DDA, DIA & targeted

Connect precursor selection, isolation windows, and chromatographic sampling to the evidence recorded by full scan, DDA, DIA, and targeted methods.

FOUNDATIONAL GUIDE · OCT 2026
ENZH
Before you begin: Mass analyzers & resolution, Fragmentation & tandem MS

Acquisition is a sampling decision

An acquisition method is a schedule for asking the instrument questions: which ions should enter a measurement, which should be fragmented, and when should each measurement repeat? The schedule determines the evidence available afterward. Start with the analytical question, sample complexity, separation, and required quantitative performance.

This Guide uses LC-MS examples, where analytes elute over a limited time. The same selection ideas appear in other experiments, but a chromatographic peak-width calculation does not automatically apply to infusion, imaging, or a different separation. The examples below are constructed teaching models, not validated instrument methods.

MS level, scan, and cycle answer different questions

  • MS level: in common instrument notation, MS1 is the first stage of mass analysis; a product-ion MS2 measurement selects a precursor population, reacts it, and measures the resulting ions. Selecting an MS2 product for another reaction and measurement leads to MS3. These labels describe stages along an ion's analysis pathway. [3]
  • Scan or measurement event: one scheduled acquisition, such as a survey spectrum or a product spectrum. The word scan does not require every analyzer to sweep through masses in the same way.
  • Acquisition cycle: a repeated set of events. One survey plus ten MS2 events can be one cycle. Ten MS2 events are not MS10, and the second cycle is not MS2.

A precursor-selection stage need not itself produce a saved full MS1 spectrum. For example, targeted MS/MS can isolate a programmed precursor without a new survey before every target. Review the actual event sequence rather than inferring it from the label MS/MS. [2] [9]

Full scan is bounded coverage

Here, full-scan MS1 means measuring the configured m/z range without deliberately isolating and fragmenting a precursor for that event. “Full” does not mean every molecule in the sample: ionization, polarity, transmission, range, and detection all restrict the result. An MS1 spectrum may still contain in-source fragments, especially with fragmenting ionization conditions. See the ionization Guide.

A series of MS1 spectra can support isotope inspection and precursor-level chromatograms. It does not by itself assign a unique structure. Also, full scan describes a measured range: a method can acquire a full product-ion spectrum at MS2. Always state both the MS level and the selection method.

DDA: select from what the survey shows

A common DDA loop records a survey, finds eligible precursor signals, ranks them, then acquires product spectra for selected candidates. Eligibility can depend on intensity, charge, inclusion lists, or exclusion rules. In an intensity-ranked Top N method, N limits the number of dependent events; it does not promise N identified compounds. Time-limited variants instead fit dependent events into a specified cycle budget. [4]

A low signal may never become eligible or may lose priority to other signals while it elutes. Small changes in coeluting signals can therefore change which precursors receive MS/MS. Conversely, a selected isolation window can contain more than one analyte: DDA does not guarantee a chemically pure product spectrum. [6]

Dynamic exclusion changes eligibility

Dynamic exclusion temporarily prevents a recently selected precursor from triggering another dependent event according to a repeat count, matching tolerance, and exclusion duration. It can free events for other precursors. It does not remove that ion from the sample, erase its survey signal, or guarantee that it was identified correctly. [5]

Constructed selection exercise: four eligible signals A, B, C, D remain ranked in that order. A Top 2 cycle selects A and B. If both are excluded for the whole next cycle, that cycle selects C and D; without exclusion, it would select A and B again. This assumes no other candidates, no rank changes, and no other filters. In practice, exclusion trades repeat evidence against opportunities for other ions; choose its duration in relation to elution and the experimental aim.

DIA: repeat a coverage scheme

In a common windowed DIA method, the instrument steps through predefined precursor intervals and records a product spectrum for each, then repeats. No individually detected survey peak needs to win a Top N selection. The original SWATH-style study demonstrated extracting peptide evidence from these systematically collected fragment data. It is one implementation, not a definition of every DIA design. [1]

Several coeluting precursors can occupy one interval and generate mixed product signals. A fragment m/z alone does not identify its source precursor. Analysis can combine compatible fragment masses, their elution profiles, precursor information when available, and measured or predicted spectral evidence. Coelution supports an assignment but is not proof by itself. Acquiring a window does not guarantee detectable or identifiable evidence for every analyte in it. [1]

Three different meanings of window

  • An isolation window selects a precursor m/z interval in the instrument before an MS/MS event.
  • An extraction window selects already recorded m/z values in software when making a trace.
  • A retention-time window determines when a target is scheduled or where a chromatographic peak is sought.

Narrower precursor isolation may reduce coisolation, but can also reduce transmitted signal; it cannot separate ions with indistinguishable m/z. Real isolation edges have a transmission profile, not an ideal on/off boundary. The isolation width is distinct from the resolving power used to measure products. [7]

For fixed-width DIA, covering the same precursor span with narrower nonoverlapping windows needs more events. Variable-width and overlapping schemes also exist; document the actual list of boundaries. The rectangular, nonoverlapping windows used below are an arithmetic simplification, not an instruction to omit overlap on a real instrument.

Targeted acquisition: SRM/MRM and PRM

Selected-reaction monitoring (SRM) records specified product-ion signals from specified precursor ions. Monitoring multiple products from one or more precursors is called multiple-reaction monitoring (MRM). The usual precursor m/z → product m/z pair is a transition. “Multiple” does not mean that ordinary MRM is MS3. [8]

In a typical triple-quadrupole implementation, Q1 selects the precursor region, collisions produce fragments, and Q3 selects the product region for each transition. Parallel-reaction monitoring (PRM) instead acquires a product spectrum for a selected precursor using a high-resolution analyzer; suitable product traces are selected during processing. “Parallel” refers to products recorded together, not every target precursor being measured at once. The recorded range and detection limits still apply. [9]

Scheduling targets near expected retention times reduces the number active at once. The scheduled interval must allow for the whole relevant peak and retention-time drift; an overly tight interval can miss it. [10] Validate target identity, interference, calibration, and precision with appropriate standards and controls. Neither a programmed transition nor a PRM spectrum automatically makes an assay selective or quantitative.

From spectra to chromatograms

A spectrum displays signal against m/z for a measurement. An extracted ion chromatogram (XIC or EIC) follows selected recorded m/z signal against chromatographic time. [12] A total-ion chromatogram sums a much broader set of recorded signals, whereas a base-peak chromatogram follows the largest signal in each selected spectrum. [13]

State whether a trace uses MS1 or MS2, its polarity, extraction tolerance, and any precursor-window filter. Changing a software extraction tolerance does not change which ions were cofragmented. A smooth extracted peak can still contain interference; software cannot create an unrecorded measurement.

Worked example: budget the cycle

Constructed serial timing model: cover precursor m/z 400–800 with equal, nonoverlapping windows. Every cycle has one 0.20 s survey, one 0.04 s measurement block per DIA window, and 0.20 s of additional cycle overhead. All blocks occur in sequence with no overlap. The per-window block is the assumed occupied time, not merely a maximum injection-time setting. These invented times describe no particular instrument.

Number of windows = (800 − 400) / window width
Cycle time = 0.20 s + number of windows × 0.04 s + 0.20 s
Approximate opportunities across the peak = peak width / cycle time

Under these assumptions, halving the width doubles the window count, but does not double the whole cycle because the 0.40 s survey-plus-overhead contribution is unchanged. The first case has 21 spectra per cycle, yet any one DIA window is revisited only once per cycle. Counting all spectra would overstate that window's temporal sampling.

If the planning target is about 10 opportunities across 12 s, the cycle budget is 1.20 s. Keeping 40 windows and the same survey and overhead would leave (1.20 − 0.20 − 0.20) / 40 = 0.020 s per window. Whether such blocks are feasible, and whether their ion statistics suffice, requires an actual method test. This is not a recommendation to shorten every acquisition.

A cycle is an opportunity, not a guaranteed target measurement

A different constructed DDA schedule might occupy 0.20 + 10 × 0.09 + 0.10 = 1.20 s. It also provides about 10 survey opportunities across 12 s, but a particular precursor might receive zero, one, or several MS2 events depending on selection and exclusion. Do not call those 10 guaranteed MS2 points for every target. A 3.00 s revisit interval would give the original rough estimate of 12 / 3.00 = 4 opportunities.

For a targeted serial example, 40 active transitions with 20 ms dwell and 5 ms pause per transition occupy 40 × (20 + 5) = 1000 ms. Doubling the active transitions gives 2000 ms if those times stay fixed. Holding 1000 ms instead leaves 1000 / 80 − 5 = 7.5 ms dwell per transition. This excludes any other overhead; dwell is the time assigned to measuring that transition. [11]

Check the real event timestamps

Real instruments may overlap ion accumulation with detection, stop accumulation before its maximum, or add switching, transfer, calibration, and other overhead. A transient duration or maximum injection-time setting is therefore not automatically the whole scan time. Parallel acquisition in hybrid instruments is a documented example of why simply adding every configured time can be wrong. [7] [14]

The ratio of peak width to revisit time is a planning estimate. Actual point counts depend on sampling phase, usable events, and the width convention. A 12 s FWHM is not a 12 s baseline width. Inspect timestamps and target traces, including crowded elution regions; test peak integration and precision rather than treating a fixed number of points as universal acceptance.

Design the experiment around the question

  1. Specify the evidence: precursor features, exploratory product spectra, systematic fragment traces, or a defined quantitative target panel?
  2. Specify coverage: ionization and polarity, precursor and product ranges, isolation boundaries, target list, retention-time schedule, and activation conditions.
  3. Check time allocation: peak-width convention, active windows or targets, accumulation and detection settings, exclusion rules, and observed revisit intervals.
  4. Test with controls: blanks, appropriate standards and quality-control samples, carryover checks, and documented injection order. Use randomization or blocking when needed to reduce confounding with drift.
  5. Evaluate the result: identification confidence, interference, missing evidence, quantitative bias and precision, and behavior across the intended samples.

Compare modes using matched sample amounts, instrument time, and evaluation criteria. Full scan can be sufficient for some feature-level questions; DDA, DIA, and targeted methods allocate time and selectivity differently. None provides a universal ranking of sensitivity, coverage, or correctness. Record the method and analysis version so another reader can understand both what was acquired and what was later extracted.

Check your understanding

Does one MS1 plus twenty MS2 events mean MS21?

No. It can be one cycle containing 21 recorded spectra. MS level describes the analysis pathway, not the number of events in a cycle.

Does dynamic exclusion remove a precursor's MS1 peak?

No. It changes eligibility for dependent selection. The ion can remain visible in survey scans while excluded from another MS/MS trigger.

Does DIA mean every molecule will be identified?

No. Range, ionization, detection, interference, and analysis limits still apply. Repeated window coverage is not complete molecular identification.

Would narrowing an extraction window repair a broad isolation window?

No. Software may reject some recorded interfering m/z values, but it cannot undo cofragmentation or separate identical product signals merely by narrowing tolerance.

Why are there only ten opportunities for one window in the first timing case?

The window appears once in each 1.20 s cycle. Twenty other spectra in that cycle do not add observations of that same window. The ratio 12 / 1.20 is approximate temporal coverage, not an identification count.

Beyond the basics

Next, connect these acquisition choices to feature finding, identification, quantification, and confidence. More advanced designs may add variable or overlapping DIA windows, ion mobility, adaptive scheduling, or additional MS stages. Read their actual event and selection logic before applying this Guide's simple arithmetic. Keep acquired evidence, extracted evidence, and inferred identity separate.
