Molecular networking: read connections as evidence

Understand spectrum and feature nodes, build interpretable MS/MS edges, and separate structural relationships from ion-identity links.

FOUNDATIONAL GUIDE · OCT 2026
ENZH
Before you begin: Spectral library search: from query to defensible match, From raw data to reliable results

A map of comparisons, not a list of identified molecules

A molecular network organizes MS/MS observations as nodes connected by selected spectral relationships. It helps ask which unknowns resemble an annotated feature, which families vary across samples, and what should be examined next. A connection is evidence under a comparison rule. It is not a chemical bond, a demonstrated biosynthetic reaction, or proof of either endpoint's structure.

Read the graph's legend before its layout. What is a node? What qualifies as an edge? What does size or color encode? Without those answers, a visually prominent cluster can be mistaken for abundant chemistry, high confidence, or a pathway even when it represents none of them.

Classical and feature-based networks count different things

In classical GNPS networking, closely similar MS/MS scans can be clustered into consensus spectra; each retained consensus becomes a node. Clustering parameters therefore influence how many nodes appear. A node is not automatically one distinct neutral compound, and spectrum counts should not be treated as concentrations. [1]

Feature-based molecular networking (FBMN) starts from detected and aligned LC-MS features, with representative MS/MS linked to feature identifiers. A chromatographically separated isomer can remain a separate node even when its spectrum resembles another feature. This preserves separation information that spectrum-only clustering can merge. It does not resolve isomers that the underlying measurements and processing failed to separate. [2]

For FBMN, keep the feature table and spectral export synchronized. Verify that each spectrum identifier maps to the intended row, precursor, and retention time. Record how representative spectra were selected or merged and what happened to features lacking usable MS/MS. The exported intensity may be a peak area or another quantity; specify its meaning before interpreting node sizes. [3]

Build the edge rule explicitly

Compare processed spectra under a named similarity measure, with declared fragment tolerance, intensity treatment, and matched-peak requirement. A modified alignment can additionally compare products shifted by the precursor difference; review the charge and ion assumptions explained in analog search. Shared fragments can suggest shared chemistry, but common low-information products and mixed spectra can produce misleading similarity.

The final graph is often further filtered. GNPS supports a minimum similarity, minimum matched peaks, and reciprocal TopK: an edge survives only if each endpoint ranks the other among its K closest candidates. Component-size limits can remove additional edges. These choices shape the picture; a retained edge and a scored pair are not identical objects. [1]

Save candidate pair scores as well as final edges when possible. Inspect representative spectra before propagating a label. Two nodes connected through a chain need not have similar endpoint spectra: A–B and B–C do not imply A–C. A connected component can therefore contain uncertainty that grows as one moves away from an experimentally supported annotation.

Add ion-identity links as a separate evidence layer

One neutral molecule can form several adducts or other ion species whose fragmentation differs enough to leave them disconnected. Ion identity networking groups candidate ion forms using coelution, chromatographic peak-shape relationships, and mass relationships consistent with specified charge, adduct, or multimer hypotheses. Ion identity molecular networking combines these relationships with MS/MS-based connections. [4]

Keep edge types visible. A proposed same-neutral relationship and a similar-fragmentation relationship answer different questions. Collapsing an ion-identity group can simplify a graph, but retain its original feature membership and ion assignments. Summing member intensities does not automatically produce a calibrated molecular abundance. The GNPS workflow offers both collapsed and uncollapsed representations. [5]

A hypothetical four-feature network

Constructed teaching example: A has m/z 301.100000 and B has 323.081945. Both are singly charged positive ions, coelute at 5.0 min, and show similar chromatographic shapes. Under [M+H]+ for A and [M+Na]+ for B, their estimated neutral masses are 300.092724 Da using rounded corrections of 1.007276 and 22.989221 Da. This numerical agreement supports testing the same-neutral hypothesis, not accepting it from mass alone. [6] [7]

Let A–B MS/MS similarity be 0.30, A–C be 0.82, C–D be 0.79, and A–D be 0.40. Assume each of these comparisons meets an illustrative six-matched-peak requirement, and all other pair scores are below 0.70. At a hypothetical 0.70 edge cutoff, A–C and C–D qualify, but A–B does not. B can still connect to A through a separately labeled ion-identity edge.

With reciprocal TopK = 1, C prefers A to D, so C–D disappears even though its score exceeds 0.70. With TopK = 2 it can remain. Neither setting changes the measured spectra. If A has a defensible library annotation, C becomes an interesting related candidate; it does not inherit A's exact identity. D remains an indirect neighbor, and B's proposed ion form needs its own checks. All numbers and thresholds here are invented for teaching.

Read missing edges and annotations cautiously

  • No edge: inspect whether MS/MS was acquired, survived filtering, and met pair and neighborhood rules before inferring chemical dissimilarity.
  • Many annotations: an extensively represented library family may appear better understood than equally abundant but poorly represented chemistry. Report library coverage and unmatched nodes.
  • Strong grouping: coeluting unrelated compounds, poor peak boundaries, or incorrect adduct assumptions can misgroup features. Review raw traces and alternative explanations.
  • A stable-looking picture: layout distance is not a calibrated structural distance. Node placement can change without any change in the edge list.

Compare justified nearby parameter settings and record which conclusions survive. Sensitivity analysis exposes dependence on graph construction; it is not a substitute for structural validation. Choose follow-up standards, targeted MS/MS, or improved separation to test the specific uncertainty.

Export evidence, not only an image

Preserve input files, feature-to-spectrum mapping, processing versions, all edge rules, edge types, node attributes, library hits, and unresolved alternatives. Report whether counts refer to spectra, features, ion-identity groups, or supported compounds. Keep blanks and sample metadata traceable. These records make a network useful even when most nodes remain unknown.

Check your understanding

Why can B join A despite their low MS/MS similarity?

A separate ion-identity hypothesis uses chromatographic and ion-mass evidence. That edge must not be presented as a high spectral similarity or a unique structural identification.

Does losing C–D at TopK = 1 show that D is chemically unrelated?

No. The edge was removed by a neighborhood rule. Its score and spectrum did not change, and absent edges have several possible causes.

References & further reading

  1. GNPS. Molecular Networking documentation: clustering, network and filtering options.

    Official parameter definitions checked 7 October 2026. Supports spectral consensus nodes, minimum matched peaks, reciprocal TopK filtering and component-size limits. Displayed defaults are not identification guarantees.

  2. Nothias et al. (2020). Feature-based molecular networking in the GNPS analysis environment. Nature Methods 17, 905–908.

    Primary publisher metadata and indexed author manuscript introduction and methods checked. Supports feature-based nodes, quantitative feature tables, and preserving chromatographically or mobility-resolved isomers. Does not imply that unresolved isomers are separated computationally.

  3. GNPS. Feature-Based Molecular Networking workflow documentation.

    Official input and workflow documentation checked 7 October 2026. Supports matching feature identifiers across quantitative and representative-spectrum exports, and explicit graph-generation parameters. No software-version-specific default is prescribed.

  4. Schmid et al. (2021). Ion identity molecular networking for mass spectrometry-based metabolomics in the GNPS environment. Nature Communications 12, 3832.

    Primary publisher-indexed introduction, results and workflow details checked; a later direct open encountered an authentication redirect. Supports connecting ion species using chromatographic and MS1 relationships, distinct from MS/MS similarity edges, and adduct-dependent fragmentation. Ion grouping is not independent proof of a chemical structure.

  5. GNPS. Ion Identity Molecular Networking workflow documentation.

    Official documentation checked 7 October 2026. Supports explicit edge types and separate uncollapsed and collapsed network representations; the original feature-to-node mapping must remain available.

  6. NIST. CODATA recommended values of the fundamental physical constants: proton mass in u and electron mass in u.

    Official constants table. Examples round the proton mass to 1.007276467 Da; u and Da name the same mass unit.

  7. NIST. Atomic Weights and Isotopic Compositions for Sodium.

    Neutral sodium-23 relative atomic mass 22.9897692820; the example subtracts one electron mass to approximate sodium-cation mass.
