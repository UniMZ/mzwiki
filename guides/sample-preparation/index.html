Guide 09 · Methods

Sample preparation & recovery

Choose a preparation strategy and separate analyte loss, matrix-dependent response, and overall process response using matched comparisons.

FOUNDATIONAL GUIDE · OCT 2026
ENZH
Before you begin: What mass spectrometry measures, How molecules become ions

Preparation changes the measurement

Sample preparation connects the material collected to the material injected. It can remove interfering components, enrich an analyte, change its chemical form, or lose it. A strong MS signal therefore does not prove that the original sample was represented faithfully. Start with the measurand: which substance or class, in which material, reported per which original amount? Measuring a free compound, total compound after release, intact protein, and peptide after digestion are different questions.

This Guide explains decisions and diagnostic comparisons, not a laboratory recipe. Use approved handling procedures for the actual material, equipment, and hazards. All numerical examples are constructed teaching data.

Choose what to keep and what to remove

The sample matrix is everything accompanying the analyte. An acceptable preparation balances recovery, cleanliness, stability, reproducibility, and the intended detection range. The Agilent sample-preparation handbook describes physical clarification, extraction, concentration, and chemical treatment as distinct operations; a workflow may combine them. [1]

  • Dilution or clarification: may reduce loading or remove particles, but does not selectively remove all dissolved interferents.
  • Protein precipitation: removes precipitated proteins while leaving many soluble constituents. A clear supernatant is not necessarily free from phospholipids or other MS-active matrix material. [2]
  • Liquid–liquid extraction: uses different distributions between liquid phases. Analyte chemistry determines which fraction matters.
  • Solid-phase extraction: uses retention and release on a sorbent to separate selected material. Unwanted loss can occur before, during, or after retention. [1]

There is no universally best choice. In targeted work, selective enrichment can be useful; in discovery work, it can exclude whole chemical classes. Write down the expected coverage before judging a cleaner-looking chromatogram. If a workflow intentionally converts proteins into peptides, describe the conversion and the inference back to protein abundance rather than calling peptide recovery “protein recovery.”

Track losses across the whole path

Transfers, surfaces, separation, concentration, and reconstitution all create opportunities for a changed result. Surface binding can be important for peptides, for example, but the appropriate container or additive depends on the analyte and method. [3] Test compatibility; do not assume a material advertised as low-binding solves every loss mechanism.

Keep original sample amount, aliquot fraction, final volume, and subsequent dilution distinct. An extract twice as concentrated does not necessarily represent twice as much original analyte. Constructed mass balance: an aliquot contains 100 ng; preparation retains 75 ng in a final 0.50 mL. The extract contains 150 ng/mL. A fourfold final dilution gives 37.5 ng/mL, even though the original aliquot still supplied only 100 ng. These are amount/volume calculations, not instrument calibration results.

Stability is a separate question from efficient extraction. ICH M10 calls for evaluating stability under conditions representing sample handling and storage. Its scope is bioanalytical assays supporting drug-development submissions; it is not a universal rulebook for every MS experiment. [4] Record storage history and handling changes so an observed difference can be investigated.

Separate recovery from matrix effect

Recovery concerns analyte retained through specified preparation steps. A matrix effect changes response because other sample constituents are present. In electrospray, coeluting material can reduce response even when analyte amount is unchanged; experimental work on biological extracts links suppression to nonvolatile solutes and droplet properties. That is one mechanistic contribution, not a complete explanation for every matrix effect. [5]

A neat standard compared only with a prepared sample mixes these questions. Matuszewski and colleagues established experiments that distinguish matrix-dependent response from extraction recovery. [6] A useful three-set comparison is:

  1. A, neat: analyte in a matrix-free reference solution.
  2. B, post-preparation: blank matrix processed first, then analyte added.
  3. C, pre-preparation: analyte added to blank matrix before the specified preparation.

Match the nominal analyte amount reaching the final comparison, final solvent composition, injection conditions, and response definition. B and C should contain comparable extracted matrix. The post/pre-spike comparisons and matrix-factor definition are illustrated in Waters' technical reference. [3] Here, assume no endogenous analyte or unresolved interference, linear response, and adequate blank correction. Without those assumptions, the simple ratios can mislead.

Worked example: a small signal has two causes

Constructed matched responses in arbitrary area units
SetA: neatB: post-preparationC: pre-preparation
Area1000800600
  • Matrix factor: MF = B / A = 800 / 1000 = 0.80
  • Extraction recovery: 100 × C / B = 100 × 600 / 800 = 75%
  • Overall process response relative to neat: 100 × C / A = 60%

The response is reduced by 20% in extracted matrix, and the preparation comparison gives 75% recovery. The combined response is 0.80 × 0.75 = 0.60 of neat. Calling the 60% ratio “extraction recovery” would conceal the response change. These definitions require the matched comparisons above; the arithmetic does not verify that a real experiment met them.

Now imagine B = 1200 and C = 900, with A unchanged. Recovery is still 75%, but MF = 1.20 and the overall response is 90% of neat. A result close to the neat response can hide opposing effects. Reporting one apparent percentage cannot resolve them.

What an internal standard can establish

An internal standard is useful only for changes it actually tracks. Added after preparation, it cannot have experienced earlier extraction loss. Added beforehand, it still requires evidence of similar behavior. A labeled analogue may help, but timing, separation, interference, and chemical stability remain relevant. Compare the analyte's matrix factor with the standard's rather than assuming compensation.

Constructed illustration: analyte MF = 0.80 and standard MF = 0.80 give a normalized factor of 1.00. If the standard's MF instead equals 1.00, the normalized factor remains 0.80. A stable ratio in one matrix does not establish behavior across all samples. Inspect individual responses as well as ratios: both signals can become too weak for reliable measurement.

Turn comparisons into evidence

Evaluate relevant concentrations and representative matrices, then inspect variation rather than only an average. A reproducible preparation with incomplete recovery can be usable when the intended measurement is properly calibrated and validated; ICH M10 explicitly does not demand 100% extraction recovery. [4] This does not excuse an unexplained loss or guarantee sufficient sensitivity.

Save the preparation map, standard-addition timing, volumes and dilution factors, raw A/B/C responses, blank treatment, and reasons for exclusions. Include a procedural blank to investigate introduced signals and appropriate repeated controls to examine preparation variability. Repeated injection of one extract tests a different step from independent preparation. See blanks and carryover, LC separation, and reliable data analysis for the next parts of the evidence chain.

Check your understanding

Does a clear extract prove that matrix effects are absent?

No. Dissolved constituents may remain and alter ionization response. Appearance does not replace a matched response comparison.

Does a 90% prepared-to-neat response establish 90% recovery?

No. In the second constructed example, 75% recovery combined with a matrix factor of 1.20 produces that 90% response.

References & further reading

  1. Agilent Technologies. Sample Preparation Fundamentals for Chromatography. Publication 5991-3326EN.

    Official manufacturer handbook, cover and full text checked. Used for categories of preparation and general LLE/SPE principles, especially chapters 2, 7 and 10. No product ranking, universal assay requirement, or detailed hazardous procedure is adopted.

  2. Waters (2020). Simple Approach for the Removal of Phospholipids in Small Molecule Quantitative Bioanalysis Using Ostro Sample Preparation Plates.

    Official application note, full text checked. Used narrowly for the fact that protein precipitation can leave soluble matrix constituents, including lipids. Product superiority claims, assay performance, and experimental recipes are not generalized or reproduced.

  3. Waters (2018). Bioanalytical Sample Preparation and Method Development for Therapeutic and Endogenous Peptides. Reference card 720006298.

    Official technical reference, full PDF checked. PDF pages 10–12 distinguish pre-extraction, post-extraction and neat comparisons and define recovery and matrix factor; page 2 discusses surface binding. Used for conceptual comparisons only, not peptide-specific preparation recipes or blanket container recommendations. A/B/C numbers in the wiki are original constructed examples.

  4. FDA / ICH (2022). M10 Bioanalytical Method Validation and Study Sample Analysis. Final guidance.

    Official final FDA PDF, November 2022, checked. Sections 3.2.6, 3.2.8 and 7.3 support carryover assessment context, stability evaluation and recovery consistency. Scope is bioanalytical assays supporting drug-development submissions. No universal acceptance limits for unrelated MS applications are claimed.

  5. King, Bonfiglio, Fernandez-Metzler, Miller-Stein & Olah (2000). Mechanistic investigation of ionization suppression in electrospray ionization. Journal of the American Society for Mass Spectrometry 11, 942–950.

    Primary experiments on biological extracts support a role for nonvolatile solutes and changed droplet properties in ESI suppression. The article does not generalize this into the only suppression mechanism. Publisher metadata and abstract verified.

  6. Matuszewski, B. K., Constanzer, M. L. & Chavez-Eng, C. M. (2003). Strategies for the assessment of matrix effect in quantitative bioanalytical methods based on HPLC-MS/MS. Analytical Chemistry 75(13), 3019–3030.

    Primary study. Author, title, volume, pages, DOI and abstract verified through PubMed PMID 12964746 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12964746/); ACS full-text retrieval was unavailable. Abstract supports distinguishing matrix effect and true recovery; exact teaching-ratio definitions are independently verified in the official Waters technical reference. No claim of full-paper inspection.
