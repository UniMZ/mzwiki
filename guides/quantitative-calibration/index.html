Guide 12 · Methods

Quantitative measurement & calibration

Turn response into concentration with a defined calibration model, suitable standards, validated limits, and explicit reporting units.

FOUNDATIONAL GUIDE · OCT 2026
ENZH
Before you begin: What mass spectrometry measures, Acquisition: full scan, DDA, DIA & targeted

A signal becomes a quantity through a model

A mass spectrometer reports ion signals. To report a concentration, connect a specified response to a specified measurand: the quantity intended to be measured. For example, “compound X in plasma, in ng/mL” is different from “X signal per milligram of total protein.” Define the denominator before comparing samples. A fold change in response is not automatically a fold change in concentration, especially across different compounds.

Calibration establishes a relationship between standards and indications, then uses that relationship to obtain measurement results. It is not the same operation as adjusting the instrument. A calibration curve describes the response–quantity relationship; the curve alone does not describe all measurement uncertainty. [1] [2] This Guide concerns concentration calibration, distinct from the mass-axis calibration used to assign accurate m/z values.

Choose the response and reference

First identify what will enter the calculation: an integrated precursor signal, one or more product-ion signals, or a ratio to an internal standard. Use the same definition for standards and unknowns. Keep integration boundaries and interference checks consistent; a precise number for the wrong peak remains the wrong measurement.

An internal standard is added to each measured sample and provides a reference response. A stable-isotope-labelled version of the analyte is often useful, but its suitability, isotopic purity, and possible interference still need testing. ICH M10 discusses these checks for regulated drug bioanalysis. Its scope and acceptance rules should not be transferred wholesale to exploratory omics. [3] Ratios can compensate for variation shared by analyte and standard; they cannot correct an effect that changes only one of them.

Worked example: invert the calibration

Constructed teaching example: assume a validated linear response over 1–10 ng/mL in the measured solution. Let y = analyte area / internal-standard area and let c be concentration in ng/mL. The stipulated relationship is y = 0.020 + 0.050c. The intercept is dimensionless and the slope has units mL/ng. The rows illustrate that equation; they are not enough to validate a real method.

An unknown gives y = 0.320, so c = (0.320 − 0.020) / 0.050 = 6.0 ng/mL. If the original sample was diluted fivefold using an independently validated dilution procedure, its concentration is 5 × 6.0 = 30 ng/mL. The curve applies to the diluted solution; the final reported result refers to the original sample. Record both values and the dilution factor.

Ignoring the intercept would give 0.320 / 0.050 = 6.4 ng/mL, a 6.7% overestimate relative to the stipulated 6.0 ng/mL. A nonzero intercept therefore matters even with a perfectly straight relationship. Conversely, a response above 0.520 would lie outside this example's supported range. Do not silently extend the line and describe the answer as validated.

Test the model, not only the correlation

A high coefficient of determination, R2, does not establish model adequacy. Inspect residuals, meaning measured responses minus model predictions, across concentration and acquisition order. Curvature suggests that a straight line misses structure; increasing residual spread suggests nonconstant variance. A broad concentration range can produce a visually impressive correlation while low-level estimates remain poor. [4]

Weighted regression can accommodate changing variance when the weights are supported by evidence. Choosing 1/c or 1/c² by habit does not verify the corresponding error model. Estimated weights can themselves be unreliable with few replicates. [5] Evaluate back-calculated standards and separately prepared quality controls rather than selecting whichever fit makes the unknowns look most plausible. Keep the model, weighting, exclusions, and accepted range in the audit record.

Separate detection from quantification

The limit of detection concerns reliable detection under a defined decision procedure. The limit of quantification concerns quantitative performance at the lower end of the method. A visible peak does not establish that its concentration is sufficiently reliable. VIM makes detection explicitly dependent on false-positive and false-negative probabilities. [7]

ICH Q2(R2) describes, among several approaches, linear-response estimates DL = 3.3σ/S and QL = 10σ/S. Here σ is an appropriate response standard deviation and S is the slope. [6] In a separate constructed example, σ = 0.003 and S = 0.050 mL/ng give DL = 0.198 ng/mL and QL = 0.600 ng/mL. These are model-based estimates, not new validated limits for the earlier 1–10 ng/mL example. Low-level samples and a declared validation plan must establish the usable limit.

Report what the number supports

Repeated agreement describes precision under specified conditions; it does not eliminate bias. [8] For an invented reference concentration of 10.0 ng/mL, measurements 9.0, 9.1, and 8.9 ng/mL average 9.0 ng/mL. They cluster closely but their mean is 1.0 ng/mL below the reference, or −10%. This demonstrates why repeat injections alone cannot establish quantitative accuracy.

Report the analyte definition, units, sample denominator, response, standards, calibration range, dilution, quality-control outcome, and uncertainty or validated performance statement. Retain below-range and above-range flags instead of converting them automatically to zero. Identity confidence, false discovery rate, and concentration uncertainty answer different questions.

Check your understanding

Two analytes have identical areas. Are their concentrations equal?

Not from those areas alone. Each analyte can have a different response relationship. You need suitable analyte-specific calibration, or evidence that a proposed shared model is valid.

Does dividing by an internal standard produce an absolute concentration?

No. It produces a response ratio. A calibrated relationship and the relevant sample-volume or mass factors are still needed to express concentration.

References & further reading

  1. JCGM. International Vocabulary of Metrology, VIM3, entry 2.39: calibration.

    Official definition distinguishes establishing and using a measurement relationship from instrument adjustment. Concentration-calibration examples are independently constructed.

  2. JCGM. International Vocabulary of Metrology, VIM3, entry 4.31: calibration curve.

    Official definition and note: a curve relates indication and measured quantity value but does not itself supply measurement uncertainty.

  3. ICH (2022). M10: Bioanalytical Method Validation and Study Sample Analysis. Final version adopted 24 May 2022.

    Official final guideline, sections 1.3, 3.1, 3.2.4–3.2.7. Supports internal-standard suitability, calibration-model reporting, independent QC preparation, and validated dilution. Its regulated drug-bioanalysis scope is stated; numerical regulatory acceptance thresholds are not generalized to exploratory omics. ICH copyright acknowledged; explanations are original and are not ICH-endorsed translations.

  4. NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods, section 4.4.4: How can I tell if a model fits my data?

    Official statistical guidance supporting residual analysis and the inadequacy of R-squared alone for model validation. The source dataset and figures are not reproduced.

  5. NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods, section 4.1.4.3: Weighted Least Squares Regression.

    Official discussion of nonconstant error variance, relative precision weights, and uncertainty in estimated weights. No automatic concentration-based weighting rule is prescribed.

  6. ICH (2023; error corrections 2025). Q2(R2): Validation of Analytical Procedures.

    Official final guideline with 2025 error corrections, sections 3.2.3.3–3.2.3.5: linear-response DL/QL estimates and subsequent performance validation. Its pharmaceutical analytical-procedure scope is not treated as a universal omics rule. EMA current-version listing was checked on 2026-10-06; the ICH-hosted final PDF was read. ICH copyright acknowledged; original teaching explanations are not ICH-endorsed translations.

  7. JCGM. International Vocabulary of Metrology, VIM3, entry 4.18: detection limit.

    Official definition links detection capability to the measurement procedure and both false-presence and false-absence probabilities. No numerical error probabilities are inferred from the teaching example.

  8. JCGM. International Vocabulary of Metrology, VIM3, entry 2.15: measurement precision.

    Agreement among replicate measurements under specified conditions; not a synonym for accuracy.
