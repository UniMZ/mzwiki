Guide 18 · Applications

Small-molecule annotation: from features to identity

Build ion, formula, and structure hypotheses, compare reference evidence, and report what remains unresolved.

FOUNDATIONAL GUIDE · OCT 2026
ENZH
Before you begin: From raw data to reliable results, Fragmentation & tandem MS

Annotate the evidence before naming the molecule

A small-molecule annotation is a proposed relationship between a measured signal and a chemical explanation. The explanation may be an ion form, a molecular formula, a compound class, or a particular structure. These are different claims. A workflow should preserve the strongest defensible claim without filling unresolved details with a familiar database name. This Guide develops the identity layer introduced in data analysis; all numerical observations below are constructed teaching examples.

Start with a question that can be tested: “Does this feature support this candidate under these measurement conditions?” Replacing that question with “What is the first database hit?” hides both alternative candidates and the limitations of the measurement.

First establish what the signal represents

Inspect the underlying chromatographic trace, isotope envelope, blanks, and MS/MS scan rather than trusting a result-table row alone. An adduct ion, an isotopologue, and an in-source product can generate different features from the same compound. Conversely, unresolved compounds can contribute to one feature. Keep feature evidence separate from molecule summaries, as the mzTab-M results model does. [1]

Record polarity, charge, proposed adduct, monoisotopic-peak choice, retention time, and the isolation window. For mixed spectra, ask which product ions actually follow the selected precursor. A fragment observed in the same scan is not automatically evidence for every coisolated candidate. Reacquisition with a narrower window or an altered separation may test the association.

Build a formula hypothesis with explicit assumptions

A molecular formula specifies atom counts, not their connectivity. Convert the proposed ion form to the appropriate neutral composition before searching. Restrict elements only when justified by the sample and experiment; a carbon/hydrogen/oxygen-only search cannot discover a nitrogen-containing answer. Isotope information, chemically plausible element ratios, and valence rules can narrow the list. Heuristic filters are prioritization tools, not guarantees that an excluded unfamiliar compound cannot exist. [2]

Constructed calculation: assume a singly protonated ion, [M+H]+, of neutral C6H12O6, using 12C, 1H, and 16O. Its calculated monoisotopic neutral mass is 180.063388 Da and calculated m/z is 181.070665 after rounding. These values use the linked atomic masses and proton mass. [3] [4] [5] [6]

If observed m/z is 181.070846, the signed error relative to the rounded theoretical coordinate is (181.070846 − 181.070665)/181.070665 × 106 = +1.00 ppm. A ±5 ppm search accepts that match; its half-width here is approximately 0.000905 m/z. Acceptance means the observation is compatible with this hypothesis under that tolerance. It neither proves a unique formula nor distinguishes the structures sharing the formula. Reassigning the peak as a sodium adduct would require a different mass correction and a new candidate search.

Use a spectral library as a comparison experiment

A spectral library contains reference spectra and associated metadata. MassBank records, for example, connect spectral data with chemical and experimental information. [7] Choose references compatible with the ionization method, polarity, precursor form, fragmentation method, and collision conditions. An EI spectrum is not an interchangeable reference for an ESI tandem spectrum.

Examine the matched peaks, their relative intensities, unmatched substantial peaks, and whether the candidate explains the chemically informative fragments. Preserve the accession, library version, score definition, preprocessing, mass tolerances, and candidate list. A score of 0.95 is a number on an algorithm's scale; it is not automatically a 95% probability of correct identification. A high score based mostly on a common fragment may distinguish a class poorly, and the correct candidate may be absent from the library.

State confidence and structural specificity separately

The MSI chemical-analysis recommendations distinguish identified compounds, putatively annotated compounds, putatively characterized classes, and unknowns. For a previously known metabolite, their level-1 proposal calls for at least two independent, orthogonal properties matched to an authentic compound analyzed under identical conditions. A literature or external-library match alone does not meet that proposal. [8]

Name the confidence framework and the evidence used rather than reporting an unexplained level number. Also state structural specificity: a formula, connectivity assignment, and stereochemical assignment are not equivalent. Comparing a standard's retention time and MS/MS can strengthen a claim, but the method must actually distinguish the relevant alternatives. Two enantiomers may remain indistinguishable in an achiral workflow. More digits in m/z do not supply missing stereochemical evidence.

Turn competing candidates into the next experiment

Suppose the constructed C6H12O6 feature has two plausible isomer candidates, A and B. Both pass the mass filter and share the dominant MS/MS products. A library ranks A first, but B has no spectrum at the acquired collision energy. The honest result is a candidate annotation with unresolved isomerism, not exclusion of B.

Next, measure authentic A and B using the sample's chromatographic and MS/MS method. If both standards coelute and remain spectrally indistinguishable, the extra measurements have exposed a method limitation. If they separate reproducibly and the sample matches A under validated matching criteria, the assignment becomes more specific. If the sample contains both peaks, report both supported components; do not compress the mixture into the original single name. Choose follow-up measurements for their ability to discriminate the remaining candidates.

Make uncertainty reusable

A useful record includes a stable feature identifier; measured coordinates; ion assignment; formula and structure candidates; supporting spectra and standards; confidence framework; unresolved alternatives; and the reason for accepting or rejecting each important claim. Retain unknowns with traceable signals. They can become interpretable when a reference is added without pretending that an annotation was already established.

Check your understanding

A formula is unique within the search settings. Is the compound identified?

No. The settings may omit the correct formula, and even the correct formula can describe several isomers. Inspect the settings and add evidence that distinguishes structures.

Beyond the basics

Continue with lipid structural evidence for naming claims at different structural depths, or ion mobility and CCS for another measurable constraint. Additional measurements are useful when they test an uncertainty, not merely when they add another score.

References & further reading

  1. HUPO-PSI and MSI. mzTab-M 2.0 metabolomics results specification.

    Official released format specification. Sections 5.2 and 5.4 distinguish samples/assays/runs and missing-data encoding; sections 6.3–6.5 distinguish molecule summaries, features, and identification evidence. A format declaration does not verify biological independence or determine the missing-data mechanism.

  2. Kind and Fiehn (2007). Seven Golden Rules for heuristic filtering of molecular formulas obtained by accurate mass spectrometry. BMC Bioinformatics 8, 105.

    Primary method paper. Supports formula filtering with isotope, element, and chemical constraints; no benchmark success rate or universal exclusion rule is adopted.

  3. NIST. Atomic Weights and Isotopic Compositions for Carbon.

    Representative carbon-13 fraction 0.0107 and carbon-13 mass 13.00335483507 Da. Teaching models hold these inputs fixed; natural sample compositions vary.

  4. NIST. Atomic Weights and Isotopic Compositions for Hydrogen.

    Neutral hydrogen-1 relative atomic mass 1.00782503223, used in the water example.

  5. NIST. Atomic Weights and Isotopic Compositions for Oxygen.

    Oxygen-16 and oxygen-18 relative atomic masses 15.99491461957 and 17.99915961286, with representative isotope compositions.

  6. NIST. CODATA recommended values of the fundamental physical constants: proton mass in u and electron mass in u.

    Official constants table. Examples round the proton mass to 1.007276467 Da; u and Da name the same mass unit.

  7. MassBank consortium. About MassBank.

    Official description of a reference spectral library and chemical/experimental record metadata. No coverage or accuracy benchmark is inferred.

  8. Sumner et al. (2007). Proposed minimum reporting standards for chemical analysis. Metabolomics 3, 211–221.

    MSI Chemical Analysis Working Group reporting recommendations. Supports evidence-specific identification of previously characterized metabolites and calibration/validation reporting. Publisher metadata/abstract, indexed PMC text, and an institutional author manuscript (Fiehn Lab, UC Davis), p. 12, were checked. The institutional manuscript is not the final typeset version. Historical numerical validation thresholds are not adopted.
