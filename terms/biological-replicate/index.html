Biological replicate

Replication at a stated biological level, whose independence follows the sampling and intervention design.

TERM ENTRY · OCT 2026
ENZH

Definition

A biological replicate represents repeated sampling or generation at the biological level relevant to a question. The label alone does not establish statistical independence. Specify the biological entity, sampling process, treatment assignment, and any shared groups. Lazic and colleagues distinguish biological, experimental, and observational units precisely because these levels can differ. [1] The number of tubes or raw files cannot settle the replicate count.

Constructed hierarchy

Eight independently sampled donors each supply one sample. Each sample has two separately prepared aliquots, each injected three times. The resulting 8 × 2 × 3 = 48 injections describe eight donors and 16 preparations. For a donor-level comparison, those 48 injections are not 48 independent biological replicates. If intervention was assigned to a higher-level group, even the donor count may not equal the number of experimental units.

Independence follows the design

Technical repeats help assess lower-level variability; repeated time points characterize trajectories. Neither automatically adds independent biological units. Conversely, an independently treated aliquot can be an experimental unit for an aliquot-level question without becoming a new donor. Preserve the hierarchy and use analysis suited to it. [2] Report counts at each level and explain which units support the inference; see experimental design and replication.

References & further reading

  1. Lazic, Clarke-Williams & Munafò (2018). What exactly is N in cell culture and animal experiments? PLOS Biology 16, e2005282.

    Original methodological study defining biological, experimental, and observational units and conditions for genuine replication. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pbio.2005282. Unit hierarchy is applied to independently constructed MS examples; study prevalence figures are not generalized.

  2. NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook of Statistical Methods, section 5.5.5: How can I account for nested variation (restricted randomization)?

    Official guidance distinguishing hierarchical variation and experimental-unit levels. Does not prescribe one statistical model for all biological studies.
